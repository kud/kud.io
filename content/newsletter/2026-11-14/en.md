## Today’s focus

### 01 · AVIATION · How a copper wire grounded American aviation {#item-01}

On Friday 13 November no aircraft could take off from the continental United States for more than four hours, from mid-afternoon Eastern time. It was only the third nationwide ground stop in the history of the FAA (the Federal Aviation Administration, the US civil aviation regulator), and the longest since the attacks of 11 September 2001. More than 8,000 flights were cancelled on Friday and more than 4,000 by late Saturday morning, according to the tracking site FlightAware, cited by the Guardian. On Saturday the transport secretary, Sean Duffy, gave the cause: modernisation work, not an attack.

**The idea**

A ground stop is an order to halt departures: aircraft already in the air carry on and land, but nothing new leaves. The FAA imposes one when it can no longer guarantee safe traffic, in this case because its en-route control centres, which track aircraft between airports, had a “communication issue”, according to Duffy. He said a contractor, L3Harris, was replacing copper lines with fibre-optic cable at a remote FAA site; a router being worked on then flooded other air traffic control systems with data and disrupted communications with aircraft. Once that site was isolated, the systems came back. Duffy says he is trying to establish whether it was “incompetence” or faulty equipment. The FAA’s head, Bryan Bedford, says he sees “zero evidence of anything nefarious” and nothing linked to artificial intelligence. L3Harris says it had completed more than 70% of the work before the incident and is supporting the investigation.

**Key dates**

- **11 September 2001** · The first nationwide stop: after the attacks, the FAA halted all civilian departures and ordered aircraft in the air to land; commercial flights did not resume until 13 September.
- **11 January 2023** · The second: take-offs were halted for under two hours after the outage of the Notice to Air Missions (Notam) system, which sends safety information to pilots.
- **13 November 2026** · The third: more than four hours; take-offs resumed at 8.35pm Eastern time.

**Did you know?**

According to Duffy, the copper wire being replaced was so old that it was being phased out. He warns that similar failures will happen again unless Congress funds the modernisation of air traffic control, for which Donald Trump is asking 30.1 billion dollars.

Italy’s data protection authority (the Garante) fined Meta, Facebook’s parent company, €805 million on Saturday 14 November for accessing Android users’ photos and videos without their “specific” consent. ## Explainer

### 08 · An “eliminated” disease {#item-08}

**Why it's in the news.** According to the Wall Street Journal, as reported by DW and Courrier international, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO, the World Health Organization’s office for the Americas) is to strip the United States of the measles-elimination status it has held since 2000; the official announcement is expected on 17 November. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the US public health agency, has recorded 5,182 confirmed cases this year in 45 states, more than double 2025’s total, according to DW.

**What it is.** “Eliminated” is not “eradicated”: the virus can always enter a country with a traveller. A country has eliminated measles when no local chain of transmission has lasted 12 months or more, with surveillance good enough to prove it. PAHO calls transmission “endemic” when the same virus, of the same genotype and lineage, circulates without interruption for 12 months or longer in one country. An independent commission of experts reviews the epidemiological and genetic evidence, and PAHO’s director decides. For example, ten imported cases in a year change nothing; a single outbreak that does not die out for a year does. To regain the status, a country must again stop transmission for at least 12 months.

### 10 · ASTRONOMY · Around a young star, ice from before it was born {#item-10}

The James Webb Space Telescope has detected water and carbon dioxide ice in the debris disc around Beta Pictoris, a star about 20 million years old and 64 light years away where planets are still forming, according to a study led by Sarah Betti of the Space Telescope Science Institute and reported by Ars Technica. The water ice is almost entirely amorphous, a disordered, glass-like solid (under 7% crystalline), and the CO₂ is intimately mixed in, at roughly one molecule for every ten, a proportion close to that of comets in our Solar System. The researchers conclude that the ice probably formed in the interstellar medium, before the star itself. If that is the rule, the ingredients of water-rich worlds may be common around young stars.

### 11 · To cross a border, first imagine it {#item-11}

At 19, Shahram Khosravi, now an anthropologist at Stockholm University, was held in a small prison on the border between Iran and Pakistan, among smugglers, undocumented workers and refugees. In this essay for Aeon he argues that wherever people are confined and displaced, a “fugitive imagination” emerges: a practical knowledge that thinks the world beyond the borders of states. A smuggler interviewed in prison told him he designs “a journey, like an architect designs a building”. Political philosophy rooted in lived experience, which makes you look at borders from the side of those who cross them.

### 12 · The philosopher of “yes, but” {#item-12}

Philosophie Magazine (in French) revisits Jean Wahl (1888-1974), a major figure of post-war intellectual life who is now largely forgotten. The philosopher Bernardo Tavares, who wrote his thesis on him, traces a life that ran from the Sorbonne to the Drancy internment camp, and a way of thinking that, convinced reality overflows our ideas, welcomed the most varied viewpoints to test their limits. A fine way into a philosophy of productive doubt, far from closed systems.
