## Today’s focus

### 01 · INFRASTRUCTURE · Planes grounded, Outlook silent: two outages during network work {#item-01}

On Friday 13 November the FAA (Federal Aviation Administration, the US civil aviation regulator) halted all departures nationwide at 3.36pm Eastern time and lifted the stop only at around 8.20pm, according to CBS News. More than 9,000 flights were cancelled on Friday, according to the tracking site FlightAware, cited by CBS. The day before, on Thursday 12 November, Microsoft’s Outlook, Teams and Copilot went down across several continents, into Friday.

**The idea**

In both cases the official explanation points to work on the network, not an attack. According to the US transport secretary, Sean Duffy, quoted by CBS, a contractor was switching “old copper wire” to fibre while replacing a router; the investigation has yet to establish whether human error or the equipment was to blame. The Department of Homeland Security says it has “no indications” of a cyber-attack. Microsoft, for its part, attributes its outage to a network capacity upgrade issue that surfaced after a recent change, “not related to cyber or AI”, according to the Irish news site TheJournal. The shared mechanism, offered as a line of inquiry rather than a conclusion: when a system funnels enormous traffic through a single infrastructure, routine maintenance can be enough to stop the whole thing.

**Key dates**

- **11 September 2001** · After the attacks, the FAA imposes the first nationwide ground stop.
- **January 2023** · Another nationwide stop, the last before Friday’s, according to CBS.
- **13 November 2026** · Nearly five hours without a departure, going by the times CBS gives.

**Did you know?**

According to the FAA’s head, Bryan Bedford, about 70% of the air traffic control network already runs on fibre. Duffy says Congress gave him 12.5 billion dollars for the modernisation; the request was raised to 30 billion late on Friday, according to CBS.

**Further reading**

In The Conversation, the researcher A. R. E. Taylor (University of Exeter) reads the Microsoft outage as a wake-up call: so many institutions relying on so few providers makes digital resilience urgent.

### 02 · UKRAINE · Nato for Donbas: an American idea, Zelensky says {#item-02}

US negotiators raised a Ukrainian withdrawal from Donbas, the industrial east largely occupied by Russia, in exchange for membership of Nato, the Western military alliance, Volodymyr Zelensky said in remarks made on Friday 13 November and released by his office on Saturday. He had met Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, Donald Trump’s envoys, in Dublin on Thursday, after their meeting with Vladimir Putin in Moscow. Washington has not confirmed. According to DW, the Ukrainian president says he is ready to put any comprehensive deal to a referendum. Why it matters: Nato membership and territory would be on the same negotiating table.

### 03 · FRANCE · A fifth Saturday against the cost of living {#item-03}

For the fifth weekend running, protesters marched across France on Saturday 14 November against the high cost of living, called out by the gilets jaunes (yellow vests) coordination and youth organisations. There were 106,000, including 24,000 in Paris, according to the interior ministry, and 200,000, including about 100,000 in Paris, according to the youth organisations. The ministry reported 219 arrests and ten police officers lightly injured. Beyond the numbers, what stands out is the staying power: five Saturdays in a row, and the coordination is already calling for an “act VI” on Saturday 21 November, franceinfo reports.

### 04 · PRIVACY · Italy fines Meta €805 million {#item-04}

Italy’s data protection authority (the Garante) has fined Meta, Facebook’s parent company, €805 million for accessing Android users’ photos without their “specific” consent. The mechanism at stake is a quiet one: a single permission, granted once to an app, is not consent to every use the app later makes of it. According to the BBC, around 20 million users are affected and Meta has 60 days to comply. The company disputes the decision and will appeal.

## Explainer

### 05 · An “eliminated” disease {#item-05}

**Why it's in the news.** The United States may lose its measles-elimination status: a commission of experts from PAHO (the Pan American Health Organization, the World Health Organization’s regional office for the Americas) is reviewing its case this month, after a postponement from April to November. The CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the US federal public health agency) had counted 5,412 confirmed cases in 2026 as of 12 November, against 2,289 in the whole of 2025.

**What it is.** Eliminating is not eradicating: the virus can always arrive with a traveller. What matters is that no local chain of transmission takes hold. PAHO calls transmission endemic when one strain, of identical genotype and lineage, circulates without interruption for 12 months or more. An independent commission reviews the data and reports to PAHO’s director, who announces the decision. For example, ten imported cases in a year change nothing; a single outbreak that does not die out for a year costs the status. To regain it, a country must again interrupt transmission for at least 12 months.

### 06 · To cross a border, first imagine it {#item-06}

“In 1986, I was 19 and behind bars in a small prison on the border between Iran and Pakistan.” So opens Shahram Khosravi’s essay for Aeon, published on Friday 13 November; he is an anthropologist at Stockholm University. He argues for a “fugitive imagination”: the capacity of people who are confined or displaced to envision and live a life beyond the worlds that seek to contain them. A smuggler tells him: “I design a journey, like an architect designs a building.” Why click: political philosophy written from inside its subject is rare.
