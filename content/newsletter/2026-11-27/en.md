## Today’s focus

### 01 · SECULARISM · Canada's top court upholds Bill 21 — and still says it discriminates {#item-01}

The Supreme Court of Canada on Friday 27 November confirmed the validity of the Act respecting the laicity of the State, the law known as Bill 21, passed by Quebec's National Assembly in June 2019, which bars public employees in positions of authority — teachers, judges, police officers, prosecutors — from wearing religious symbols at work. In a 532-page judgment (_English Montreal School Board v. Quebec (Attorney General)_, 2026 SCC 41), the court declares the law "valid and operative" with one exception: section 8, which requires officials to perform their duties with their faces uncovered, is of no force or effect as it applies to members of the National Assembly, because it would prevent them from sitting — a conclusion more theoretical than practical today, since no sitting Quebec legislator wears a face covering. The appellants, chief among them the English Montreal School Board, have no further appeal.

**The idea.** Everything turns on section 33 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, the notwithstanding clause. It allows a legislature to shield a law, for a renewable five years, from the rights set out in sections 2 and 7 to 15 of the Charter: freedom of religion, freedom of expression, the right to equality. In 1982 it was the compromise that persuaded the provinces to accept the patriation of the constitution. Quebec has invoked it six times since 2018, twice for Bill 21: in 2019, then again in 2024. So the central question in the case was not "does this law respect rights?" but "may a legislature decide in advance that it need not?". The court's answer is yes: pre-emptive use is lawful, following the 1988 _Ford_ decision, and the override's exercise is "not subject to any limit other than those expressly set out in the provision's text". For Chief Justice Richard Wagner, Bill 21 belongs to "Quebec's legitimate and historic intention to secure the laicity of the State in a free and democratic society".

**Did you know?** The judgment is nonetheless split, and that is where it gets interesting. By four votes to three, the court holds that judges retain the power to declare a law inconsistent with the Charter even while the override is in force: a declaration with no invalidating effect, but which states publicly what a law costs in rights. Four of the seven judges wrote that Bill 21 was, in the abstract, discriminatory. Yet no declaration was issued, for want of a majority behind the same order: Justice Sheilah Martin, joined by two colleagues, considered that the ban breached section 28 — the guarantee of sexual equality, which she holds immune to section 33 — and wanted a binding order rather than a mere declaration. Her Quebec colleague Nicholas Kasirer did not join her. Seven of the nine judges heard the case, from 23 to 26 March 2026, in one of the longest hearings in the court's history: a record 51 interveners, and five provinces alongside Quebec. The court also found that the law does not infringe section 23, which protects the English-speaking minority's education rights, while accepting that it constrains that minority's hiring decisions.

The reactions map the fracture. Prime Minister Mark Carney said his government would study the ruling's implications, adding that it mattered for all legislatures "to respect the rights and liberties of all Canadians, including those in the minority". Stephen Brown, chief executive of the National Council of Canadian Muslims, called the law "discriminatory": "Entire categories of people are now barred from entire sectors of employment, not because of what they have done, but because of who they are." The chair of the English Montreal School Board said the board was "obviously very disappointed", while taking "solace" from the court's declarations. The leader of the Bloc Québécois read the ruling as confirming Quebec's right to protect its values. The Parti Québécois, which supports the law, has governed since the election of 5 October; Quebec also widened its secularism rules this year to subsidised daycares and post-secondary institutions, and restricted public prayer on pain of fines.

**Further reading**: the court's plain-language "Case in Brief" and the full reasons are both public.

### 02 · EUROPEAN UNION · Twelve hours of summitry, and still no budget for 2028-34 {#item-02}

The 27 leaders left Brussels on Thursday night without agreement on the multiannual financial framework for 2028-34, after twelve hours of talks from which four countries — Denmark and Austria among them — walked out partway through. The proposal on the table brings the budget down to €1.582tn, including €140bn to repay pandemic-era joint borrowing, roughly €220bn to €230bn below the nearly €2tn the European Commission had proposed. That is not enough for Germany and the net contributors who fund almost 40% of the budget and want spending concentrated on defence, competitiveness and innovation; it is too much for France, Italy and the "friends of cohesion", with Emmanuel Macron calling any reduction in long-term spending a "strategic mistake". The second blockage is an extra €45bn loan to Ukraine, split between non-military and military support, opposed by Slovakia, the Czech Republic and Hungary; Bulgaria has agreed to fund the non-military part. Chancellor Friedrich Merz made the budget conditional on a deal among all 27 — "The European Union comprises of 27 member states, not 23 or 24" — and saw "new movement" towards a smaller envelope, though "we are not there yet". Every country holds a veto, and the calendar matters: a deal before the year is out is what would allow the implementing legislation to pass in 2027 and the money to reach beneficiaries in January 2028.

### 03 · WAGES · France's minimum-wage experts warn against a country of minimum earners {#item-03}

The expert group on the Smic, France's statutory minimum wage, consulted every year before the wage is uprated, delivered a report on Friday that remains opposed to the government's discretionary rise — the first since July 2012 — four days before it takes effect. According to the report, as reported by the news agency AFP, "while a discretionary rise in the Smic may be a political answer to a legitimate concern about purchasing power, the analyses documented in this report do not support recommending one for that purpose". The 1 December increase is 1.6%, of which 1% covers inflation and 0.6% is discretionary; across 2026 the Smic will have risen 5.3% in all, against "barely 2%" for other wages, which the experts say "could further compress the distribution of wages". The magnitudes they put forward are large, and mostly concern tax: extending the freeze on the scale of employer social-contribution reliefs at minimum-wage level, as the government's 2027 budget bill envisages, would in their estimate cost 110,000 jobs a year, or about 125,000 full-time equivalents once the Smic rise is added. Jobs near the minimum wage are concentrated in firms with fewer than 50 employees, where 61% of posts pay below 1.05 times the Smic, and in hospitality, retail and support services. The group recommends refocusing the general contribution reliefs on wages up to twice the Smic, down from three times today, with an extra allowance for workers under 25 — whose unemployment rate is 21%, against 8.2% for the workforce as a whole — and revising the automatic uprating formula to take in forecast inflation and the movement of wages above the minimum. These are recommendations: the government decides, by decree.

### 04 · CLIMATE · What if CO₂ were counted where it comes out of the ground? {#item-04}

An analysis published on 17 November by Carbon Brief reassigns historical CO₂ emissions to the country that extracted the coal, oil or gas rather than the one that burned it. Over 1850 to 2025, using a dataset from Robbie Andrew, a senior researcher at the Norwegian climate institute CICERO, the league table shifts markedly: Russia, Saudi Arabia and Australia emerge as the largest CO₂ exporters of all time. Russia is credited with an extra 69 billion tonnes from exported fuels; Saudi Arabia's cumulative total more than quadruples, making it the fifth-largest contributor to warming; Australia's share more than doubles, putting it in the top ten. The United States and China still lead overall, but both have been net importers since 1850; Germany, India, France and Turkey slide down, and Japan falls from sixth on a territorial basis to 34th. Per head, the leaders are Brunei, Kuwait, Qatar, Norway and Trinidad and Tobago. This is not official accounting, and its author says so: the dataset is a draft release, and responsibility is "joint responsibility between extractors, producers and consumers". Its value lies elsewhere — it makes the supply side visible, the side climate negotiations talk about least.

### 05 · Ultralight dark matter {#item-05}

**Why it's in the news.** More than 90% of the universe's matter and energy is unaccounted for — "that's a bit embarrassing", as the astrophysicist Atsushi Taruya, of Kyoto University, puts it. With the heavy particles long favoured by the field still undetected (WIMPs, or weakly interacting massive particles), physicists have been building a different kind of experiment, surveyed on 25 November by Quanta Magazine, to hunt candidates at the opposite end of the mass scale.

**What it is.** Ultralight dark matter means hypothetical particles of extraordinarily small mass — the axion, the leading candidate, would weigh between 10⁻¹² and 10⁻⁹ of an electron's mass. At those masses the thing stops behaving like a tiny ball and behaves like a wave: a field oscillating through space and washing through the Earth. So the search is not for an impact but for a tremor. At the University of Washington, the ADMX experiment puts a resonator in a magnetic field to turn an axion into a photon (nothing found so far across much of the lighter mass range). At DESY, Germany's particle-physics laboratory, ALPS II sends light through a 300-metre wall by converting it into axion-like particles and back again. In Vienna, Thorsten Schumm's team exploits "a freak-of-nature coincidence", two almost exactly identical energy levels in the thorium-229 nucleus, to build a nuclear clock that has already tightened the constraints on how such matter might couple to photons and the strong force. In Leiden, a magnet levitated in a superconducting trap simply sits "waiting for something to knock it around". A Japanese team, meanwhile, pulled 65 signals consistent with ultralight dark matter out of ten years of readings from a Scottish magnetometer: nothing conclusive without worldwide data. The method is the point: when a hypothesis yields nothing, change the instruments before changing the conclusion.

### 06 · AI & BIOLOGY · OpenAI says its general models design proteins. Read the numbers {#item-06}

OpenAI published a post and a preprint on 24 November arguing that the GPT-6 family has an "intrinsic" protein-design capability: designing protein backbones and sequences through reasoning and general-purpose coding tools, with no specialised biology model generating the designs. The company says an internal research model matches or beats specialised models on a number of fourteen tasks, and that designs can be steered in plain language (attachment site, structural class, number of helices). The published figures repay a close reading, because they are both real and modest: of 322 minibinders tested in the lab with its partner Onava against five targets, 35 actually bound, a 10.9% hit rate — and OpenAI itself notes that the reference RFdiffusion study reported higher rates. Redesigning sequences on known structures, success ranges from 85% (75 of 88 for MDM2) down to 14% (8 of 59 for PD-1); on thirty tasks from the MotifBench suite the internal model claims 43.7% success against 27.8% for RFdiffusion, though that result is presented as preliminary and purely computational. Above all, candidates were filtered using specialised models (Boltz-2, OpenDDE, ESMFold2), so end-to-end design without a dedicated model is not established; predicting structure from sequence remains well behind specialised predictors; some benchmark scores may reflect memorisation; and binding to a target is only one step — specificity, stability, activity and safety were not tested. This is a vendor's claim, documented, with its limits written down.

### 07 · Zero, and not "almost one" {#item-07}

A one-page site keeps a counter: the number of diseases cured by artificial intelligence. It reads zero, and spells it out — "Zero. Not 'almost one.'" Its criteria are explicit and strict: the AI must have found both the target and the molecule, a regulator must have approved the therapy, and patients must be cured, the disease eliminated or durably reversed. Reading scans, predicting protein structures or speeding up lab work does not count. The page honestly lists the nearest candidates — Insilico Medicine's rentosertib, in phase III since September 2026; Takeda and Nimbus's zasocitinib, with a decision due in the first quarter of 2027 from the FDA, the US drug regulator; Isomorphic Labs, still pre-clinical — and sets them against the public predictions of the industry's leaders. Its author is not named and the counter is a static snapshot rather than a live feed, so read it as an argument, not an official register. A useful counterweight to the story above.

### 08 · Windows 98 in a browser tab {#item-08}

No disk image, no source port: an x86 interpreter and a reimplementation of the Win32 API, written directly in WebAssembly, on which the original unmodified executables run. You will find 3D Pinball Space Cadet, SkiFree, Minesweeper, Paint, Winamp, DirectDraw and Direct3D games, and even 16-bit Windows 3.1 programs. The code is published under the MIT licence, nothing is installed and, the page says, nothing leaves the browser: settings and scores stay in local storage. On a phone, games that need keys get on-screen controls. The engineering is the real pleasure here; the nostalgia is a bonus.

### 09 · Who does the talking, and who gets criticised {#item-09}

A French site counts, day by day, who speaks on the debate programmes of thirteen television and radio channels, and what is said there about the country's political currents. Over the past thirty days, 1,850 broadcasts were logged: presenters and columnists take 59% of the speaking time — from 31% on France 5 to 78% on RMC — against 12% for people tied to a political movement. The site separates raw speaking time from "listening time", weighted by each slot's audience, and also counts judgements: the far left draws 46 substantive criticisms per 100 sentences naming it, and 70 on CNews, against 27 for all currents combined. The approach is deliberately broader than that of Arcom, the French broadcasting regulator, which counts only politicians' airtime. The site does not say who publishes it or how its classification is done, which is the limit to keep in mind: this is a measurement to argue with, not a verdict.

### 10 · Copying a SQLite database without copying it {#item-10}

`sqlite3_rsync` does for a database what rsync does for a folder: the replica sends cryptographic hashes of its pages, the origin returns only the pages that differ, or asks for finer-grained hashes. Two pleasant consequences: you can copy a snapshot to another machine over SSH without first duplicating the database locally — which both the backup API and `VACUUM INTO` require, a real problem on a small instance already more than half full — and resynchronising an existing copy afterwards costs "a few percent at most" of the database's size, unless the two have drifted a long way apart. The tool arrived as experimental in SQLite 3.47.0 in October 2024 and was enhanced in 3.50.0 in May 2025; Simon Willison writes that he only grasped its most attractive characteristic this week.

### 11 · Self-hosting, fun again {#item-11}

Jan-Lukas Else explains, in a post dated 26 November, why he has rediscovered the pleasure of hosting his own services: AI has not changed why he likes it, only how much effort it takes to get from an idea to a working thing. With his infrastructure living in Git, a coding agent can work on it like any other software project; a bespoke Debian image for his server took him about half a day, against an estimated week without help; and for a small problem, writing his own tool has often become easier than adapting someone else's. What makes the piece worth reading is that he lists his own objections: every service added needs maintaining, AI-written code can carry bugs and security holes, delegating a change does not excuse him from understanding it, and the ease of building says nothing about whether a thing is worth building.
