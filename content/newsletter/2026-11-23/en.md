## Today’s focus

### 01 · PUBLIC FINANCES · Macron’s “golden rule”: what a constitutional budget lock would really constrain {#item-01}

Emmanuel Macron is considering writing a budget “golden rule” into the French constitution, and convening Parliament’s two chambers as a joint Congress as early as January to adopt it. “If there is still volatility by January, thinking about a golden rule like the 2011 one may be an idea to explore,” he told Le Figaro, according to franceinfo, which reports that the president’s office confirmed the plan for an early-year Congress on Monday 23 November. A few months before the presidential election, the stake is more than technical: how far an outgoing president can tie his successors’ hands on public spending.

**The idea** There is no single definition of a golden rule: it is a legal norm requiring balanced public accounts, through a cap on debt, deficit or spending. The one the Élysée has in mind revives a constitutional amendment passed by the National Assembly and the Senate in 2011, under Nicolas Sarkozy, but never put to Congress. It writes no figure into the constitution. Instead it creates “framework laws on balanced public finances” that set a path for at least three years and, for each year, “a maximum of spending and a minimum of revenue” binding on budget laws and social security financing laws. A budget that broke that frame could be struck down by the Constitutional Council, the body that checks laws against the constitution. “The advantage of a golden rule is that it lets you set a medium-term timetable,” the finance minister, Roland Lescure, argued on franceinfo.

The context explains the timing. According to franceinfo, the deficit exceeds 5% of GDP, debt is close to 120% of GDP, and interest payments alone are set to pass €91bn in 2027, €20bn more than this year. The state’s ten-year borrowing rate crossed 5% in October, and the gap with Germany is widening. Simon-Pierre Sengayrac, of the Fondation Jean-Jaurès think tank, sees in the announcement “a wish to calm the financial markets”.

The disagreement is about what the rule would prevent. A constitutional amendment by this route needs three fifths of the votes cast by deputies and senators sitting together. The right, the centre and the far-right Rassemblement national back it, which would be enough without the left, which opposes it: Éric Coquerel, of La France insoumise, sees a reform that would drastically limit future governments’ room for manoeuvre. The economist Mathieu Plane, of the Institut français d’économie (formerly OFCE), warns against a “magic formula” effect and says everything depends on the detail: does the rule allow for the economic cycle, meaning recessions, and does it exclude certain investment? The Élysée says it “does not restrict governments’ freedom”, since they would set the path themselves.

**Did you know?** French local authorities are already barred from voting and running unbalanced budgets, Mathieu Plane points out. And the most cited example, Germany’s “debt brake”, written into its Basic Law in 2009 and limiting the annual deficit to 0.35% of GDP, was called into question in 2025 to fund defence and investment, franceinfo notes.

### 02 · TECH & POWER · Nvidia reinstates Jensen Huang, with the White House claiming a hand in it {#item-02}

Eleven days after resigning, Jensen Huang was back on Monday 23 November as chief executive and chairman of Nvidia, the AI chipmaker that became the world’s most valuable company in October, according to Ars Technica. The board, which voted unanimously according to DW, said it relied in part on assurances from “senior levels of the United States government” that his return poses “no national security risk”, Ars Technica reports. Huang resigned after admitting extramarital affairs, one of them, according to press reports, with a former executive of Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia customer suspected of diverting chips to China in breach of export controls, the US restrictions meant to slow Chinese AI. US intelligence sources believe she was working for Chinese intelligence, Ars Technica and DW report; the allegations are unproven, DW stresses. The board says it has handed an inquiry to independent directors and set up a committee for national security and export compliance. The shares, down about 25% since the resignation, jumped about 30% at the open.

### 04 · HOUSING · Denmark’s Supreme Court finds the “ghetto law”’s “non-Western” test neutral {#item-04}

Denmark’s Supreme Court on Monday 23 November rejected an appeal by seven families evicted from the Nøjsomhed estate in Helsingør, north of Copenhagen, where 91 leases were terminated in 2022. The 2018 law requires the share of family social housing to be cut by 2030 in deprived areas where more than half of residents are “immigrants and descendants from non-Western countries”, a statistical category covering every country outside the European Union bar a handful of Western states. The families argued this was discrimination based on ethnic origin. The court found the criterion neutral, because each of the two groups contains many origins and there was no information on the evicted residents’ ethnic background. Yet in December 2025, in another Danish case, the Court of Justice of the European Union had made clear that the mere fact that a criterion covers several origins “does not, in itself, preclude” it being linked to ethnic origin, while leaving national judges to decide. The housing minister, Jacob Mark, welcomed the ruling; according to tenants’ associations, some 11,000 people will have to leave their homes by 2030.

## Explainer

### 05 · Functionalism {#item-05}

**Why it's in the news.** Thomas Nagel, 89, author of “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” (1974), now a classic on consciousness, has just won the $1m Berggruen Prize. In an interview with Wired, he called the idea that large language models might be conscious “just ridiculous”. Meanwhile, MIT Technology Review reports, Anthropic listed the possible consciousness of its models among the risk factors in the prospectus for its stock market listing.

**What it is.** Functionalism, in the philosophy of mind, holds that a mental state, a pain, a belief, a desire, is defined not by what it is made of but by the role it plays: what triggers it, what it produces, how it relates to other states. A simple example: a pain is whatever is caused by injury, makes you believe something is wrong and makes you wince. If the role is what counts, nothing in principle stops a mind running on silicon rather than neurons. That is exactly what Nagel disputes: in his view the physical substrate is not irrelevant, consciousness probably needs something analogous to a nervous system, and “language by itself is not a natural manifestation of consciousness”. The debate remains open: in a 2020 survey cited by MIT Technology Review, 39% of 1,092 philosophers thought some future AI systems will be conscious.

### 06 · WEB · Ladybird, a browser with an all-new engine, reaches alpha {#item-06}

The Ladybird browser can be downloaded as an alpha for macOS and Linux since Monday 23 November, its creator, Andreas Kling, announced. What sets it apart is its engine, the part that turns HTML, CSS and JavaScript into a rendered page: it is written from scratch rather than forked from an existing one. Today almost the whole web runs through three engines: Blink (Chrome, Edge and most others), WebKit (Safari) and Gecko (Firefox). Since implementations are what give web standards their weight, a fourth independent engine, backed by a non-profit, is a rare counterweight. The team warns that some sites will not work, that the browser will sometimes crash, and that it makes “no promises about security” for now. For developers, it supports WebDriver, for automated testing, and the Firefox DevTools protocol, and the alpha updates itself automatically.

### 07 · BIOLOGY · Gene editing runs on the cell’s own repair kit {#item-07}

A long piece in Quanta Magazine makes a little-known point: genome-editing tools do not “rewrite” DNA themselves; they damage it, then let the cell do the repair. Some cells suffer up to 100,000 DNA damage events a day, according to the biologist Björn Schumacher. The worst is a break across both strands, which sets off a response involving more than 500 proteins. Two pathways fix it: non-homologous end joining, the more common, which glues the ends back at the risk of errors, and homologous recombination, which is highly accurate but only available between DNA copying and cell division, because it copies the intact sister chromatid. In 1994, Maria Jasin’s team showed that a deliberate break made inserting a gene by this route far more efficient; since 2012, CRISPR-Cas, guided by RNA, has made it possible to cut almost anywhere. Researchers pick the repair pathway to suit the goal: inserting a precise gene or, on the contrary, causing mutations.

### 08 · Your trackpad can talk {#item-08}

The small open-source program nospeaker makes a MacBook’s trackpad speak by hijacking its haptic motor, the one that fakes the “click”. macOS’s synthetic voice is analysed every ten milliseconds, then played back as sine tones by the motor, through a private Apple framework. The result is robotic and easier to follow if you read along, its authors warn, and a macOS update could break it. But it is a lovely demonstration of what you can get hardware built for something else to say.

### 09 · El Niño, a natural event and a human story {#item-09}

In an essay for Aeon, the environmental historian Dagomar Degroot, of Georgetown University, traces the history of El Niño, the recurring warming of the Pacific that scrambles the world’s weather, as a new episode takes hold. He tells how fishermen on the coast of what is now Peru named it after the Christ Child, because it arrives around Christmas, and why it remains so hard to forecast, or even define. His argument: El Niño has contributed to millions of deaths but made others rich, and it is “as much a human creation as a natural reality”. A long read, for looking at climate through a historian’s eyes.
