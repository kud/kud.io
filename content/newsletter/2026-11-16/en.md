## Today’s focus

### 01 · MIGRATION · Five EU countries prepare deportations to Rwanda {#item-01}

On Monday 16 November in Vienna, Germany, Austria, Denmark, Greece and the Netherlands announced the start of concrete negotiations with Rwanda to establish a “return hub” for people with no right to remain in the European Union (EU). No final agreement has yet been reached: the governments aim for the first transfers in 2027. Euronews reports that the pilot facility could accommodate around 100 people at a time. This is a proposal under negotiation, not an operating centre with settled terms.

**The idea**

A return hub is not an asylum centre: only people whose claims have been rejected, with an enforceable return decision and all appeals exhausted, would be sent there, and minors are excluded. Once there, they would be free to move around Rwanda, according to Euronews, and could then settle, go back to their country of origin or move to a country where they have the right to stay, according to Rwanda’s interior minister, Vincent Biruta, quoted by Al Jazeera. He promises “an independent monitoring mechanism”. The five countries would pay all costs; no estimate was given, and what Kigali gets in return is unclear. Denmark’s prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, calls it “a game changer”. The European Commission welcomes “meticulous preparatory work” while saying it wants human rights guaranteed, franceinfo reports. On the other side, 47 aid, trade union and rights groups are urging the five to drop the plan; Human Rights Watch says it would finance “a government responsible for serious human rights abuses”. NGOs fear it could one day target asylum seekers too, which the five countries deny.

**Key dates**

- **2022** · The UK signs a deal to send asylum seekers to Rwanda.
- **2023** · The UK Supreme Court rules it unlawful (see Explainer).
- **16 November 2026** · The European plan is announced in Vienna.
- **2027** · First transfers targeted.

**Did you know?**

To set the plan apart from Britain’s, Germany’s interior minister stresses that it covers only people already rejected, according to Al Jazeera. Euronews points out, though, that the European Court of Human Rights bars transfers to places where fundamental rights may be violated: legal challenges are expected.

## The essentials

### 02 · CLIMATE · COP31: a first draft deal, and reservations already {#item-02}

The presidency of COP31, the UN climate conference in Antalya, circulated an 18-page draft decision on Monday 16 November. It calls for faster action to move away from fossil fuels and proposes that tripling adaptation finance for developing countries should reach at least $120 billion a year by 2035. But the baseline for that tripling has not been agreed: the draft explicitly calls for one to be established. According to the UN Environment Programme (UNEP), international public adaptation finance stood at $28–32 billion in 2024, against needs estimated at around $305 billion a year by 2035. Tripling those 2024 flows would cover only 20–29% of projected needs; that percentage is not a calculation of the separate $120 billion proposal. A source from an African delegation told Anadolu that measurable finance commitments matter more than broad declarations. Chris Bowen, who chairs the negotiations, warned that not every provision would command consensus. The draft remains a proposal, not a final agreement.

### 03 · FRANCE · Marine Le Pen would end financial aid to Ukraine {#item-03}

On Monday 16 November, after meeting Ukraine’s ambassador to France, Vadym Omelchenko, Marine Le Pen, the presidential candidate of the RN (Rassemblement national, the National Rally), repeated that if elected, the state of France’s public finances would no longer allow financial aid to Kyiv. According to a party statement, she would honour existing commitments, including the EU’s €90 billion loan, and keep up equipment donations, humanitarian aid and diplomacy. For several weeks she has said she would keep paying for six months before stopping, franceinfo recalls. The day before, Emmanuel Macron’s entourage said those who want to cut aid “are playing against us, not against Ukraine”. Why it matters: she leads the Elabe poll published on 14 November, with about 32% of voting intentions, according to Euronews.

### 04 · UK · Women convicted over abortions are pardoned {#item-04}

Since Monday 16 November, women convicted or cautioned in England and Wales for an offence relating to their own pregnancy have been automatically pardoned, with nothing to apply for, the Ministry of Justice said, according to UPI. Police and courts must also delete records of arrests and investigations. The mechanism: the Crime and Policing Act 2026 removes women’s criminal liability for their own pregnancy, but leaves access to abortion unchanged; helping a woman to end a pregnancy outside the law remains an offence. How many women are affected? The government itself says it cannot put a number on it. For Heidi Stewart, chief executive of BPAS (the British Pregnancy Advisory Service, an abortion provider), the reform recognises “the profound harm caused by their criminalisation”.

## Explainer

### 05 · Non-refoulement {#item-05}

**Why it's in the news.** This is the principle that brought down Britain’s plan to deport people to Rwanda: on 15 November 2023, the UK Supreme Court ruled unanimously that there were “substantial grounds for believing” that people sent to Rwanda faced “a real risk of being returned to their home country”, Human Rights Watch reports. The European plan announced on Monday will face the same test.

**What it is.** Non-refoulement bars a state from expelling or returning a person, “in any manner whatsoever”, to a place where their life or freedom would be threatened. It is set out in Article 33 of the 1951 Refugee Convention, but it is also customary law: according to UNHCR (the UN refugee agency), it binds every state, signatory or not. It covers indirect returns and transfers to third countries. The convention allows two narrow exceptions, but the ban on returning anyone to torture or inhuman treatment is absolute. For example, sending someone to a country that is safe for them, but that will send them back to the one they fled, amounts to sending them back yourself.

## Technology & science

### 06 · LANGUAGES · Rust 1.100 names code that never returns {#item-06}

Rust 1.100, released on 12 November, finally stabilises the `!` or “never” type after a decade of discussion and several unsuccessful attempts. It describes a computation that can never yield a value, such as a branch that returns early or a function that terminates a process. Rust could often infer that behaviour already, but programmers could not name `!` everywhere in a type signature. The change makes some APIs more precise, including results whose error case is impossible. There is a subtle compatibility consequence: fallback rules change in older Rust editions, and some projects may need updated dependencies. Cargo, Rust's package manager, also gains a practical supply-chain precaution. It can delay selecting newly published package versions, for example for three days, giving maintainers and security researchers time to catch malicious releases. A cooldown is no substitute for checking dependencies, but it is a useful option.

### 07 · MOBILE & WEB · Dart 3.14 makes upgrading less awkward {#item-07}

Dart 3.14, released on 11 November, improves the tools surrounding the language used by Flutter. Its new `dart migrate` command automates parts of the move to a newer SDK: preparatory fixes, version constraints and optional adoption of features. On the web side, the WebAssembly compiler now emits standard exception-handling instructions, allowing standalone modules to run in runtimes such as Wasmtime without relying on a JavaScript host for exceptions. There are also improvements to 128-bit SIMD, a way of carrying out multiple numerical operations in parallel, with up to fourfold gains in selected benchmarks according to the Dart team. Those figures do not mean every Flutter application will become four times faster. The more useful story is a smoother migration path and more scope for running Dart code outside browsers.

### 08 · IOS DEVELOPMENT · CocoaPods stops taking new releases soon {#item-08}

CocoaPods will stop accepting new packages and versions on 2 December 2026, after a two-year wind-down, the CocoaPods team explains. The dependency manager was once central to building iOS and Objective-C applications. Existing packages will remain available in read-only form, so projects that depend on them will not suddenly fail to build. They will, however, stop receiving new releases through that channel. Teams still using CocoaPods need to check whether their libraries are available through Swift Package Manager and test migration, particularly where older dependencies or bespoke build scripts are involved. This is a change in the shape of the mobile development ecosystem, rather than merely a shutdown date.

### 09 · What if the horse made the state? {#item-09}

We are often taught that the state was born of granaries: grain surpluses, farmers to tax. In an essay published by Aeon on Monday 16 November, Lhamsuren Munkh-Erdene, a historian and anthropologist at the National University of Mongolia, argues for another origin: “transportation – not grain”. Steppe chariots, Egyptian donkeys, Comanche and Mongol riders: in his view, warbands made mobile by animals founded the first states. He also argues that the dominant theory owes much to Cold War politics. Why click: a contested thesis, but one that makes you look at history from the steppe rather than the field.

### 11 · A Rust browser for seeing how the Web works {#item-11}

A developer has built `bare`, an experimental browser engine in Rust, reimplementing components ranging from TLS and HTML parsing to CSS layout, painting and a JavaScript interpreter. Its value is not that it will replace Firefox tomorrow. It is that each layer is inspectable: when a page fails, the developer can follow the problem through the engine. Agent-driven tests use the same input path as an ordinary visitor. The creator reports 48 passing public-site journeys as of 10 November, but says this is an incomplete learning project, not a browser safe for everyday use. It is an intriguing look at the machinery behind a click.
