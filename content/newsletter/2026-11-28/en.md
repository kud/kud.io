## Today’s focus

### 01 · LAW OF THE SEA · Falklands: the sea tribunal won't stop Sea Lion, because it cannot say whose islands they are {#item-01}

The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS), the Hamburg court set up under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), on Saturday 28 November rejected, by 18 votes to 3, Argentina's request to order the United Kingdom to freeze the Sea Lion oil project. The field lies within 200 nautical miles north of the islands Argentina calls the Malvinas and the UK the Falklands. The tribunal does not say London is right on the merits: it says it has no jurisdiction to judge them.

**The idea.** On 28 September Argentina began arbitration against the UK under Annex VII of the Convention. Until that arbitral tribunal is formed, article 290(5) lets a party ask ITLOS for "provisional measures": interim orders to stop one side creating a fait accompli. Two conditions apply: the future tribunal must appear, at first sight (_prima facie_), to have jurisdiction, and the matter must be urgent. On 13 October Buenos Aires asked for a halt to any new drilling or production infrastructure and a ban on new licences. Its argument was that the dispute concerns the coastal state's rights, the duty not to exploit disputed resources unilaterally and the protection of the marine environment, not sovereignty itself. London replied that all of this first requires knowing who the coastal state is, and that the Convention contains no rule for deciding that. On each of Argentina's four claims the tribunal sided with the UK on this point: deciding them "presupposes the determination of a question of territorial sovereignty", which falls under rules of international law other than the Convention. Without apparent jurisdiction, it did not even examine urgency.

**Did you know?** The tribunal still put on record the commitments the UK made at the hearing: no drilling before 21 March 2027, no extraction before March 2028, and no new licences while the licensing regime is suspended. It "expects" both parties to apply the Convention in the disputed areas. Among the three dissenting votes was Argentina's own appointed _ad hoc_ judge, Judge Vinuesa, who wrote a joint dissent with Judge Horinouchi; Judge Patel wrote another. The project is run by Navitas (65%) with Rockhopper Exploration (35%), which took their final investment decision on 10 December 2025 and are aiming for first oil in the first quarter of 2028, according to the tribunal's press release.

The reactions show what is at stake. Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband and Attorney General Ellie Reeves welcomed a decision they present as vindicating the UK's legal defence, restated British sovereignty and the islanders' right to self-determination, and called on Argentina to end its economic pressure on the islands. The Falklands' Legislative Assembly said Argentina had failed, MercoPress reports. For Buenos Aires, which frames its claim within the UN's decolonisation process, the Convention route is closing on the issue that matters most. One question remains wide open: opening a new offshore oil field, whose environmental impact was not argued on the merits. The arbitral tribunal has yet to be formed, and the UK's commitments now set the timetable.

**Further reading**: the text of the order and the judges' opinions are published on the tribunal's website.

## The essentials

### 02 · PHILOSOPHY · Thinking costs energy, and our models of the mind forget it {#item-02}

In an essay published by Aeon on 27 November, the philosopher of science David Colaço (Munich Center for Mathematical Philosophy) and Philipp Haueis (Bielefeld University) make one charge against models of the mind, whether symbolic, computational or neural: they ignore metabolism, how the brain gets its energy, maintains itself and clears its waste. The figures they recall are telling: about 2% of body mass, 20% of its calories, roughly 20 watts, and close to 40% of that budget spent maintaining and repairing neurons rather than sending signals. Their thesis: "a model of memory is not viable if it requires a system to do more than its metabolism can support". They apply it to two debates. A synaptic memory model that settles into equilibrium describes, they argue, a dead neuron; a non-equilibrium model, powered continuously, better explains how old memories survive new learning. And the theory of "ego depletion", in which effort draws on a store of glucose, runs into both failed replications and the fact that the brain's glucose use barely changes during a demanding task. The most topical passage concerns AI: if energy constrains the computations a brain performs, whether a chip and a brain run "the same" computation is something to test, not assume. The authors concede the limit themselves: metabolism narrows the options, but it does not settle the big theories of mind.

### 03 · LOCAL ENERGY · In rural Burgundy, a village swaps heating oil for local wood {#item-03}

In Mont-Saint-Vincent, in France's Saône-et-Loire, a wood-fired heat network has been running since summer 2025, according to a franceinfo report published on 28 November. The principle: a municipal boiler house with three 200-kilowatt boilers heats water that is piped to the connected buildings — the care home and its 55 residents, the school, the gendarmerie and about twenty houses. Most of the wood comes from within 50 kilometres. The installation cost €1.87m, funded by the EU's regional development fund (FEDER), the Bourgogne-Franche-Comté region and Ademe, France's ecological transition agency; the commune paid €20,000 towards studies, and the operator Forestener runs it under a public service concession. The results reported come from the operator and users, not an independent evaluation: 224 tonnes of CO₂ avoided a year and heating 20% cheaper than oil, according to Forestener; €185,000 of expected savings over ten years for the care home; €500 to €600 a year for one couple interviewed. The limits are stated too: the subscription of about €139 a month weighs on second-home owners, and an Ademe engineer points out that wood "remains an energy with a stock to manage": cutting more than the forest grows would lose the climate benefit. The case is interesting because it is small: without grants and pooled demand, a village could not have carried the investment.

## Technology & science

### 04 · MATERIALS · Cement fired in seconds, with plasma {#item-04}

Ordinary Portland cement is made by firing limestone and clay for hours in large kilns to produce clinker, the material that hardens when it meets water. A team from Berkeley, Stanford and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory describes another route in _Nature_ on 25 November: a microwave-heated carbon plasma reaches local temperatures of up to 2,400°C, enough to liquefy the raw materials, cutting the reaction from hours to seconds. In the liquid phase the elements mix better and form reactive clinker phases; electron microscopy filmed in real time shows that their disordered surface speeds up setting. The authors also turned hydrated cement paste from demolition concrete into clinker that meets ordinary Portland cement specifications. Two caveats. The claim of "low-cost cement production at scale" rests on the authors' own techno-economic analysis, not a pilot plant. And the abstract does not quantify the emissions avoided: electric heating removes combustion, but the climate benefit will depend on the electricity used and the share of recycled material.

### 05 · OPEN SOURCE · Pimalaya: mail, contacts and calendars on the command line, now stable {#item-05}

On 26 November the Pimalaya project released stable versions of its family of command-line tools written in Rust for managing personal data: Himalaya 3.0 for mail, Cardamum 1.0 for contacts, Calendula 1.0 for calendars, Neverest 1.0 for synchronisation, Carillon 1.0 to watch mailboxes and calendars and trigger actions, and Ortie 3.0, out a few days earlier, for OAuth tokens, the authorisation protocol behind online services. The appeal is consistency: they share one configuration, one output format and one set of error codes, and each exposes an MCP server, the protocol that lets an AI agent call tools. The safeguards are cautious: Himalaya's server refuses any write without the `--allow-write` flag and any sending without `--allow-send`, and Ortie never hands the token to the agent. The same commands work over IMAP, JMAP, the Gmail API, Microsoft Graph, CalDAV or CardDAV, and a local storage format, pimdir, reaches 1.0. The project is funded by the NLnet foundation and the European Commission's NGI programme. Stated limits: no syncing from JMAP yet, search without stemming or regular expressions, and no migration guide from the 0.x versions.

### 06 · A perfectly normal language {#item-06}

PsychoScript is a small virtual machine for a subset of JavaScript, to embed in a Node or Bun app and run code you don't want to let loose: a model's, a user's or a plugin's. Nothing is reachable by default — no network, no files, no timers, no `eval` — and the script can only call the functions the host hands it. Its rare trick: a run can be suspended mid-flight, serialised to bytes and resumed later, in another process. The page plays corporate parody with a straight face — "a perfectly normal scripting language", complete with a "normal viewing" declaration form — but the documentation is serious and candid about limits: no memory cap, a regular-expression match that cannot be interrupted, and conformance with the reference JavaScript test suite not yet checked in continuous integration. MIT-licensed code.

### 07 · Colours with weight {#item-07}

In _Colorbound_, Anku, a young Aymara boy, sets out to find the members of his late grandfather's band for one last tribute. The puzzle-platformer rests on a simple, lovely idea: Anku collects colours on his palette and paints the world, and each colour changes the physics of objects — lighter ones make them float away, heavier ones make them drop. Made by the small Chilean studio Panpipe, it won the Latin American Game Showcase game jam in 2025 and draws on Andean music and art. It came out on 12 October on Windows, with French interface and subtitles among its languages; the publisher also lists console versions whose dates we could not verify. A short, gentle game rather than a challenge.
