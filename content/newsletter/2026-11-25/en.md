## Today’s focus

### 01 · SPACE · Dimorphos, four years after the crash: what the Hera probe is finding {#item-01}

**The idea.** The simplest way to nudge a threatening asteroid off course is to hit it: the “kinetic impactor”. On 26 September 2022, NASA tested it for the first time, flying its 576-kilogram DART spacecraft at 6.1 km/s (about 22,000 km/h) into Dimorphos, an asteroid about 150 metres wide that orbits a larger one, Didymos (about 730 metres). The pair posed no threat to Earth; it was chosen because a natural satellite lets you measure the effect of the impact precisely. The effect was clear: Dimorphos’s orbit around Didymos, which had taken 11 hours and 55 minutes, shortened by about 33 minutes. But from Earth, telescopes could see little more than the trail of debris, some 10,000 kilometres long. Launched in October 2024, the European Space Agency’s Hera probe arrived in October 2026 for what its principal investigator, Patrick Michel, likens to Inspector Columbo returning to the crash scene.

The first images, released this week, show one face of Dimorphos entirely transformed: where the asteroid was rounded, it is now flattened by a stadium-sized crater that looks as wide as the face that was struck. “The crater is almost as large as the body itself,” the planetary scientist Sabina Raducan, of Hera’s science team, told the BBC. The impact also changed how it moves: before DART, Dimorphos always showed the same face to Didymos, as the Moon does to Earth; it now tumbles chaotically, “like a barrel that you throw down the stairs”. Didymos shows no apparent change. Another surprise: the HyperScout-H hyperspectral camera has spotted hundreds of boulders, from tens of centimetres to a few metres across, moving around the pair. “This exceeds our best expectations,” says Julia de León of the Instituto de Astrofísica de Canarias, who also points to “weird structures” inside the crater that are not yet explained.

**Key dates**

- **26 September 2022**: DART strikes Dimorphos at 6.1 km/s.
- **October 2024**: launch of Hera, an ESA mission costing €360m.
- **October 2026**: Hera arrives at the asteroid pair.
- **3 and 6 November 2026**: HyperScout-H images the system from about 30 kilometres away and reveals the cloud of boulders.
- **June 2027**: planned end of close-up observations, with details down to a few centimetres.

**Did you know?** Gravity on the surface of Didymos is about 40,000 times weaker than on Earth, according to the researcher Naomi Murdoch, yet it is strewn with boulders the size of cars and buses, sitting on a world with almost no gravity.

What matters is not the picture but the measurement. Among other things, Hera must determine Dimorphos’s mass: that is what makes it possible to work out how effective DART’s shove really was, and so, explains the planetary scientist Simon Green, to say one day what mass of spacecraft, at what speed and how far in advance, would be needed to push an asteroid out of Earth’s path. An object the size of Dimorphos would not threaten the whole planet, but could destroy a city. The orbiting boulders complicate what comes next: the probe will have to manoeuvre through this debris to get closer, and its two shoebox-sized satellites are then due to attempt a landing.

**Further reading**: the Instituto de Astrofísica de Canarias’s release explains how HyperScout-H works out mineral composition across 25 colour bands.

### 02 · TRADE · Washington rebuilds its tariffs, this time in the name of “overcapacity” {#item-02}

The Office of the US Trade Representative (USTR) on Wednesday 25 November proposed new tariffs of up to 20% on imports from 14 economies, including the European Union, China, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam. They conclude an investigation opened in March into 16 partners accused of “structural excess capacity”: Washington argues that subsidies, state-suppressed wages and state-owned companies let their factories sell cheaply and undercut American industry. Mexico and Norway, also investigated, have been spared for now. This is more than another turn of the screw: it is the third legal basis tried to rebuild the “Liberation Day” tariffs of April 2025. In February, the Supreme Court ruled that the emergency economic powers law used at the time did not allow tariffs; the administration now relies on Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. That provision was designed to hit back quickly at one country over one industry, the trade expert Dmitry Grozoubinski told Al Jazeera: “The legislation is supposed to be a scalpel, and they used it like a flamethrower.” According to a senior official quoted by Reuters, the rates will respect negotiated caps (15% for the EU, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, 19% for Malaysia) and will not be added to sectoral tariffs on cars or steel, nor to the 10% to 12.5% duties imposed in July over forced labour: the higher rate will apply. China, however, would see 20% added to the roughly 25% left over from Donald Trump’s first term, taking some goods to 45%. The EU “strongly rejects” the premise that it is a source of overcapacity; Beijing calls it a “false issue”. The measures face a public comment period and a hearing, and would not take effect before January, according to Deutsche Welle.

### 03 · WAR IN UKRAINE · Hacked statistics put Russia’s war dead at close to half a million {#item-03}

Russia publishes no figures for its losses in Ukraine, and its statistics agency, Rosstat, stopped publishing deaths from external causes, which had been used to estimate them, two years ago. Hacking groups, Belarus’s Cyber Partisans and Ukraine’s Silent Crow, have nonetheless taken more than 11 million death records from its servers. Rosstat admits a cyber-attack but denies that any protected data leaked. A dozen media outlets, including Bloomberg, Der Spiegel and Important Stories, have analysed the files: according to franceinfo, their estimates range from 472,000 to 500,000 military deaths. BBC Russian, working with the independent outlet Mediazona, counts at least 490,334 soldiers killed since February 2022. Its method: keep only deaths in Ukraine or Russia’s border regions with an explicitly military cause (425,174 records), add those identified through employment and 51,067 people declared dead by a court, often soldiers missing in action, then exclude women and men outside the 18 to 65 age range. The files match 90% of the named list the BBC has compiled from open sources. They also show who dies: in 2025, 65% of deaths among Russians aged 18 to 24 were war-related, and men from Tuva, Buryatia and Zabaykalsky Krai, among the poorest regions, are 20 to 25 times more likely to be killed than those from Moscow or St Petersburg. Adjusting for delays in registration, Mediazona and Meduza put the figure above 700,000, a more uncertain model. The data say nothing of the wounded, and come from a hack no official source confirms. The Kremlin spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, urged people to trust only the defence ministry, which has not published figures for several years.

### 04 · ENVIRONMENT · In Iowa, the US Army blows up 4.4 tonnes of lead explosive {#item-04}

At about 9am local time on Wednesday 25 November, the US Army detonated in place 68 drums holding about 4.4 tonnes of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, in the south-east of the state. Lead azide, a compound of lead and nitrogen, is used in detonators to set off other explosives. This batch had crystallised with age, which the Army had known since March: the slightest friction, vibration or change in temperature could set it off, and moving it was out of the question. The problem is the lead, a neurotoxin that does not break down in the environment and is most dangerous to young children and pregnant women. Residents within about 8 kilometres were advised to leave, and those further out to shelter indoors. The Army’s models had the airborne lead drifting about 160 kilometres to the south-west, with fallout over some 500 square kilometres. To limit it, the two containers were packed with sand and lime, covered in fire-suppressant foam and sprayed by water cannons. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) had deployed 300 people and collected more than 1,200 air, soil, dust and water samples as a baseline; more than an hour after the blast, its real-time monitors had detected no airborne lead, according to Grist. Post-blast sampling will show where the lead settled and whether a clean-up is needed. Most residents only learnt of the operation last week; public alarm and a legal challenge by environmental groups delayed it by several days but did not stop it.

### 05 · PHILOSOPHY · Can the law make a public servant honest? {#item-05}

After the Hillsborough stadium disaster in Sheffield, which killed 97 Liverpool fans in 1989, South Yorkshire Police reviewed its officers’ statements before passing them to the inquiry: 164 were substantially changed, 116 of them to remove criticism of the force. The 2021 trial of two former officers and the force’s solicitor collapsed because there had been no legal duty of candour towards that inquiry. The Public Office (Accountability) Bill, known as the Hillsborough law and now before the House of Lords, is meant to close that gap: up to 1.9 million public servants would have to act with “candour, transparency and frankness” towards inquiries, and a new offence of misleading the public would carry up to two years in prison. A piece in The Conversation asks the question that follows: what does honesty mean once it becomes a legal duty? The Hillsborough statements need not have contained a lie: the deception lay in what had been taken out. Researchers call creating a false impression with true statements “paltering”. The bill allows for this, targeting dishonesty “whether by means of falsehood, concealment, obfuscation or otherwise”, omissions included, but it defines neither dishonesty nor what counts as “misleading”. Every statement leaves something out; an honest mistake is not a lie; and prosecutors will have to prove what an official knew and intended. The piece sets out the dilemma: draw the line too narrowly and edits like Hillsborough’s slip through again; draw it too vaguely and officials may say less and hedge everything.

### 06 · The Jevons paradox {#item-06}

**Why it matters.** Efficiency is often offered as the answer to ecological crises: leaner data centres, AI that optimises delivery routes. A piece in The Conversation recalls an argument more than 160 years old: that efficiency can instead push total consumption up.

**What it is.** In 1865, the English economist William Stanley Jevons published _The Coal Question_, on the future of British coal. In the chapter “Of the Economy of Fuel”, he wrote: “It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth.” The mechanism is simple: when a machine uses less fuel for the same work, that work gets cheaper; people then use it more, for more purposes, and total demand can outstrip the savings. The Conversation piece applies this logic to digital technology: in its author’s view, networks and computing made possible global supply chains that produce and ship more, and AI, by making companies more efficient and more profitable, pushes them to invest and expand rather than produce less. The limits: Jevons was writing about British coal in the 19th century; applying him to AI remains a thesis, which the piece argues without measuring the size of the effect. Efficiency itself is not the culprit: what counts is what is done with the gains.

### 07 · SPACE & AI · The FCC lets SpaceX launch up to a million data-centre satellites {#item-07}

The Federal Communications Commission (FCC), the US communications regulator, on Wednesday 25 November granted SpaceX conditional authorisation to deploy up to a million “Starmind” satellites, fitted with AI chips to act as data centres in orbit. According to Ars Technica, the full constellation would increase the number of satellites in orbit roughly fiftyfold. The timetable is long: SpaceX has seven years to put a first satellite into service, until November 2035 to deploy 100,000 and until 2040 for the full million. The FCC did not grant, for lack of sufficient information, the waiver that would have let satellites above 600 kilometres be sent to disposal orbits rather than brought down through the atmosphere. Groups such as the Secure World Foundation and the American Astronomical Society had raised the risks of collisions, debris, upper-atmosphere pollution from re-entries and interference with astronomy; the FCC ruled some of these questions outside its remit, launch emissions among them. The authorisation does not establish that data centres in space are technically or economically viable, Ars Technica notes. Google and Blue Origin are exploring the same idea, according to The Verge.

### 08 · SECURITY · 1Password: an employee’s GitHub account taken over, vaults untouched, the company says {#item-08}

The password manager 1Password has set out the details of an intrusion it found on 19 November. According to its own account, an attacker took over an employee’s corporate GitHub account and used it to read source code and push malicious code to internal services, which downloaded administrative data on a subset of customers: names, email addresses, account identifiers, creation and last sign-in times, public keys, parameters for the SRP authentication protocol, and tokens contained in some invitation and account-recovery emails. The attacker also copied private code and reached the continuous integration and deployment system, which holds infrastructure secrets. The company says vaults, their contents and the keys that protect them were not affected, since its design gives it no access to users’ passwords or secret keys. This was not a software flaw fixed by an update: the response was to shut the compromised account, revoke the stolen token, rotate every internal credential that may have been exposed, and lengthen the codes sent by email, without which the stolen tokens are not enough, from six digits to ten alphanumeric characters. Customers need not do anything, but 1Password advises caution with unexpected messages in its name. This is the company’s own account; it says independent experts are validating its response, but no outside report has been published so far.

### 10 · How do you beat n log n? {#item-10}

Every programmer learns that you cannot sort n items in fewer than n log n steps. Ibrahim Ahmed explains why that limit only holds in one particular model: the one where you compare two items at a time, each comparison cutting the tree of possibilities in half. His diagrams show how recent algorithms get past it by dropping that assumption, for instance by exploiting the structure of the keys rather than comparing them one by one. It asks for a little maths, but finally makes intuitive a bound most of us recite without understanding.

### 11 · At home abroad, a stranger at home {#item-11}

A Portuguese anthropology doctoral student came to Berlin for a five-month Erasmus semester and has now lived there for nearly nine years. In an essay for Psyche, he starts from the “blasé” attitude described by the sociologist Georg Simmel, the protective indifference of the overstimulated city dweller, to describe its opposite: the newcomer for whom nothing means anything yet. In 2024 and 2025 he gathered the stories of other Portuguese people in Berlin, like the young woman whose father asks whether she is glad to sleep in her childhood bed again, and who does not know what to say. A tender piece on what settling elsewhere does to the place you left.
