## Today’s focus

### 01 · SPACE · Voyager 1, one day of light away {#item-01}

At 10.16.07 UTC on Wednesday 18 November, Voyager 1 was 25,902,068,356 kilometres from Earth, according to NASA: the distance light travels in twenty-four hours. No human-made object had ever gone so far. Launched on 5 September 1977, the probe took 49 years, 2 months and 11 days to get there, travelling at nearly 61,000 km/h. To give a sense of scale, the physicist Ed Macaulay of Queen Mary University of London points out in The Conversation that it is more than 63,000 times farther away than the Artemis II crew were at their record distance in April 2026.

**The idea**

A light-day is a distance, not a length of time. In practice, a command sent from Earth now takes a whole day to reach Voyager 1, and its reply another day to come back: two days to find out whether an instruction got through. On 25 August 2012 the probe crossed the heliopause, the boundary where the wind of particles streaming from the Sun gives way to the interstellar medium, about 18 billion kilometres from the Sun. That is not the edge of the Solar System as far as gravity is concerned, but it is the limit of the bubble of plasma the Sun inflates around itself. Beyond it, particles are slower, the magnetic field is weaker and dust is about half as abundant, Science News reports. Most of the time it is “very quiet and very consistent” out there, says the physicist Jamie Rankin of Princeton University.

**Key dates**

- **5 September 1977** · Launch from Florida.
- **5 March 1979** · Closest approach to Jupiter.
- **12 November 1980** · Flyby of Saturn and its moon Titan.
- **17 February 1998** · Voyager 1 becomes the most distant human-made object.
- **25 August 2012** · Entry into interstellar space.
- **18 November 2026** · One light-day.

**Did you know?**

The probe is still measuring things nobody can explain. Its instruments have recorded four spikes in magnetic field strength and particle density; since the fourth, in mid-2020, readings have stayed high, and the cause is unknown. Jeewoo Park, a physicist at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center, suggests aftershocks from solar eruptions. Even the shape of the Sun’s bubble is still argued over: a comet for most researchers, a sphere or even a croissant for others, according to Science News. To keep going, the team switches instruments off one by one: the cameras fell silent after the famous “pale blue dot” picture of Earth in 1990, and the low-energy charged particle instrument was turned off in April 2026, EarthSky reports. Scientists estimate that its nuclear power sources, whose output is declining, leave it ten to fifteen years of contact with Earth. After that it will travel on in silence: in about 44,000 years it will pass near the star Gliese 445. “Voyager, to this day, is a multi-generational mission,” says Linda Spilker, the mission’s head scientist at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL).

## The essentials

### 02 · INTERNET · Quad9 leaves France rather than block websites {#item-02}

Quad9, a Swiss non-profit foundation that runs the public DNS resolver 9.9.9.9, will stop answering users in France on Tuesday 24 November. A DNS resolver is the directory that turns a domain name into a server address: without one, a browser can find nothing. In a case brought by beIN Sports, a Paris court ordered Quad9 to block domains streaming pirated matches, under a penalty reduced from €580,000 to €1,000 a day, according to the foundation’s announcement. The legal basis is Article L.333-10 of France’s sports code, which lets sports rights holders target anyone able to help stop an infringement, resolvers included, Tech Stackups explains. Quad9 replies that it keeps no data on its users, so it cannot tell which of them are in France; blocking for everyone would impose a French ruling on the whole world. The foundation says it faces about twenty other cases in France. There is a precedent: Cisco withdrew its OpenDNS service from the country in June 2024. Why it matters: DNS blocking is sidestepped by switching resolvers, and the measure takes a free service away from users, businesses and public bodies among them, who had pirated nothing.

### 03 · ECONOMY · The ECB worries about the bets on artificial intelligence {#item-03}

In its Financial Stability Review published on Wednesday 18 November, the European Central Bank (ECB) says that “risks from AI valuations, fiscal imbalances and geoeconomic tensions cloud the financial stability outlook”. Current share prices, it writes, “depend on very ambitious earnings expectations for AI-related firms”. Their financing needs are vast: big US tech firms are projected to invest a cumulative $5.5 trillion by 2030, and a growing share is borrowed in bond markets, where issuance linked to AI infrastructure has hit records this year. A notable part is denominated in euros, raising euro area investors’ direct exposure. The ECB also notes that AI-related stock market listings are on track to exceed the dotcom peak as a share of US GDP. Banks, for their part, lend little directly to AI and report a record-low non-performing loan ratio of 2.2% in September; but credit quality is deteriorating in their exposures to software firms and chipmakers. Why it matters: a central bank does not forecast a crash, it maps where a shock would travel, and this document draws that map.

### 04 · CHINA · A meme, Mao and the censorship Beijing exports {#item-04}

In spring 2026, in a light-hearted video about memes featuring him, Kylian Mbappé called the figure in a meme depicting Mao Zedong a “Chinese dictator”, without naming him. The video resurfaced on 26 October, the night of the Ballon d’Or. The next day three Chinese sponsors dropped him; he lost his accounts on two Chinese social networks and his place in the game FC Online; state broadcaster CCTV suspended its coverage of La Liga and demanded an apology. State media speak of “inappropriate remarks” without ever mentioning Mao, so most Chinese internet users do not know what was said. He borrows the sinologist Perry Link’s image: Chinese censorship is less a fire-breathing dragon than an anaconda coiled in a chandelier, which rarely needs to move because everyone adjusts before being asked.

## Explainer

### 05 · Rare earths {#item-05}

**Why it's in the news.** The term comes up in every debate about industrial dependence on China, often without anyone saying what it means. Marie-Odile Simonnot and Baptiste Laubie, process engineering researchers at the University of Lorraine and the CNRS (France’s national research centre), point out in The Conversation that they are “neither rare nor even earths”.

**What it is.** Rare earths are a group of 17 chemical elements: the 15 lanthanides, from lanthanum to lutetium, plus scandium and yttrium. They are relatively abundant in the Earth’s crust; what makes them precious is their very similar chemistry, which makes them hard to separate from one another. Their biggest use is in neodymium-iron-boron permanent magnets for electric car motors and wind turbines, so much so that neodymium accounts for nearly a third of demand. They also turn up in screens, MRI scanners and catalysts. The bottleneck is not the mine but the plant: China accounted for about 70% of global mining in 2023 but holds 85–90% of refining and separation capacity, which already let it squeeze supply in 2011. A French example: Solvay’s plant in La Rochelle, running since 1948, is described as the only European site processing all the rare earths in quantity, and the Caremag plant under construction near Lacq is meant to recycle magnets. Today less than 1% of rare earths are recycled.

## Technology & science

### 06 · SECURITY · nginx: an eleven-year-old flaw, exploitable without logging in {#item-06}

nginx, one of the most widely used web servers in the world, shipped fixes on Wednesday 18 November for a set of flaws disclosed by the security firm Xint under the name “NGINX2Root”. The most serious, CVE-2026-123007, is an integer overflow in HTTP/2 flow control: present since HTTP/2 arrived in nginx 1.9.5 in 2015, it lets an unauthenticated attacker run code with the nginx worker’s privileges, as long as HTTP/2 is switched on. Xint describes four more use-after-free bugs (reusing memory that has already been released) affecting HTTP/2, HTTP/3, the DNS resolver module and the mail proxy. The security company Wiz, which analysed the two HTTP/2 flaws published by F5, nginx’s owner, rates them 9.2 out of 10. The fixes are in nginx 1.31.9 and 1.30.7; Linux distributions may backport them without changing the version number shown. More worrying: chained with a Linux kernel flaw in the SCTP protocol, present since 2018, the attack gave Xint root on the host machine, even from inside a Docker container. No kernel fix was available on Wednesday. No active exploitation has been reported; until you can update, turning off HTTP/2 and stopping the `sctp` module from loading reduce exposure. A notable detail: according to Xint, four of the five flaws were found by a fully automated scan.

### 07 · OPEN SOURCE · Redict winds down and points to Valkey {#item-07}

The developer Drew DeVault announced on 17 November that he is ending Redict, the fork of the in-memory database Redis he started after Redis changed its licence in 2024. Version 7.3.6 will be the last. His explanation: Redict set out to build a community around a copyleft licence, which requires changes to be shared, but “with the valkey project’s success, and Redis’s return to a copyleft license”, it could not find a sustainable one. He recommends Valkey, the permissively licensed fork backed by the Linux Foundation, which is compatible with Redict: migrating mostly means installing the package and renaming the configuration file. He advises against going back to Redis, which requires contributors to sign a contributor licence agreement (CLA) that, in his view, makes a future licence change possible. Why it matters: two years after the split, the ecosystem is consolidating around a single community fork.

### 08 · CLIMATE · The “champagne curve”: development without ever more emissions {#item-08}

Five economists and political scientists, among them François Gemenne (HEC Paris) and Thomas Porcher (PSB Paris School of Business), present in The Conversation a finding they published in 2025 in a peer-reviewed journal. Using data from 119 countries between 2000 and 2022, they compare the Human Development Index (HDI, which combines health, education and income and is calculated by the UN Development Programme) with consumption-based CO2 emissions, meaning the carbon in imported goods is counted too. The curve rises steeply, then flattens like a champagne coupe: beyond an HDI of 0.8, extra emissions bring no significant gain in development. They argue for climate policies tailored to three groups of countries, with thresholds at 0.6 and 0.8. The limits, which the authors acknowledge: the HDI is an imperfect measure, only CO2 is counted, and the relationship varies widely from one country to another.
