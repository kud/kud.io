## Today’s focus

### 01 · JUSTICE · Myanmar found responsible for genocide against the Rohingya {#item-01}

On Thursday 19 November the International Court of Justice (ICJ), the UN’s highest court, which settles disputes between states, ruled that Myanmar had committed genocide against the Rohingya, a largely Muslim minority in Rakhine State in the west of the country. The decision was taken by nine votes to six among the fifteen judges. According to the BBC, it is the first time an international court has found that a state, rather than individuals, committed genocide. The ruling concerns the “clearance operations” the army launched in August 2017, after the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA), an armed group, attacked more than thirty police posts. The court found that around 10,000 Rohingya were “violently killed” and some 815,000 targeted in one way or another; more than 700,000 fled to Bangladesh, where around a million refugees now live in the camps at Cox’s Bazar, the BBC reports.

**The idea**

The 1948 Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, adopted after the Holocaust, does not treat every massacre as genocide: it requires proof of intent to destroy a protected group, in whole or in part. That is what makes the finding so rare. The court had ruled only twice before on claims of genocide by a state, and both times found the evidence insufficient to attribute the acts to the state, the BBC notes. This time its president, Yuji Iwasawa, said genocidal intent was “the only reasonable inference” from the evidence: executions of men, “widespread” sexual violence, “extreme violence” against children, deliberate burning of homes, attacks on civilians as they fled, and “extensive destruction of evidence”. The court acknowledged ARSA’s role but rejected Myanmar’s defence that it was fighting terrorism. The crucial point: the ICJ is not a criminal court. It rules on a state’s responsibility, not on individuals; it cannot send anyone to prison, and enforcing its rulings depends on the UN Security Council and political pressure. It ordered Myanmar to pay “proportionate” reparations, with the amount to be set later, to help refugees return safely and to investigate those responsible, according to DW.

**Key dates**

- **August 2017** · The “clearance operations” begin in northern Rakhine State.
- **November 2019** · The Gambia, backed by the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, takes Myanmar to the ICJ under the Genocide Convention.
- **January 2020** · The court orders provisional measures: prevent any act of genocide and preserve evidence.
- **July 2022** · The court rejects Myanmar’s objections; the case can be heard on its merits.
- **19 November 2026** · Final judgment, with no appeal.

**Did you know?**

The Gambia had no direct connection to the crimes: it is the first time a country has brought a case to the ICJ purely as a party to the Convention, Human Rights Watch points out. According to Antonia Mulvey, executive director of the NGO Legal Action Worldwide, it is also the first time the court has recognised rape and sexual violence as acts of genocide. The ruling will weigh on cases still pending, including South Africa’s against Israel over the war in Gaza: the barrister Philippe Sands, who helped bring the Myanmar case, said a ruling the other way would “in effect” have ended that case, though Thursday’s judgment does not decide its outcome; Israel calls the accusations “wholly unfounded”. First consequences: the UK is expelling Myanmar’s defence attaché in London and has called for a special session of the Security Council, the Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband announced, according to DW. Myanmar’s representative at the court, Ko Ko Hlaing, had “no comment”.

### 02 · GEOPOLITICS · In Shenzhen, a “peace declaration” that steps around the wars {#item-02}

On Thursday 19 November, on the sidelines of the APEC summit (the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum), Xi Jinping, Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin ended the first formal meeting between the leaders of China, the United States and Russia with a four-point “peace declaration”, released by the Kremlin and Chinese state media, franceinfo reports. In it they commit to preserving “strategic stability”, reaffirm that “a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought” and promise to “engage in dialogue” on reducing nuclear risks, without any concrete measure. They also plan to increase output of refined petroleum products, back reform of the UN and say the International Criminal Court (ICC), which tries individuals and has issued an arrest warrant for Vladimir Putin, “must not abuse” its powers. The text mentions neither Russia’s war in Ukraine nor the US-led war in Iran, DW notes. On nuclear weapons, the US–Russian New START treaty expired in February, and Beijing, with an estimated arsenal of around 500 warheads against more than 5,000 each for Moscow and Washington, still refuses to be capped on the same terms, Chim Lee, an analyst at the Economist Intelligence Unit, explains. For him, as for Emma Chanlett-Avery of the Asia Society Policy Institute, Vladimir Putin gained most: the image of an equal to the other two, despite Western efforts to isolate him.

### 03 · CLIMATE · COP31: fossil fuels vanish from the draft deal {#item-03}

The new draft decision published on Thursday 19 November at COP31, the UN climate conference in Antalya, Turkey, no longer mentions fossil fuels, DW reports. Released by Australia, the summit’s co-host, it drops two references to a transition away from coal, oil and gas, and a call to speed up their phase-out, that were in the previous version. Yet the final decision at COP28 in Dubai, in 2023, called for a gradual transition away from fossil fuels. The text keeps a target pushed by Turkey: raising electricity’s share of final energy demand to 35% by 2035, from around 20% today, without saying whether that electricity must be low-carbon. On money, it calls for further talks on tripling funding for adaptation in developing countries to at least $120 billion, and notes adaptation needs estimated at $305 billion a year up to 2035: set against those annual needs, the target would cover about 39%. Decisions are taken by consensus, and Panama has said it will block any deal without a cap on oil production and action on methane. “Insufficient,” says Jan Kowalzig, a climate finance expert at Oxfam Germany.

### 04 · HOUSING · France extends rent controls for two years {#item-04}

On Thursday 19 November the French Parliament extended rent controls for two years. The scheme caps rents in about 70 towns and cities, including Paris, Lyon, Bordeaux and Lille, and covers 1.2 million tenants, according to franceinfo. It is an experiment that was due to lapse on 24 November. The bill, tabled by the Socialist MP Inaki Echaniz, set out to make it permanent and wider; the Senate cut it back to a plain extension, which the National Assembly passed by 148 votes to 95. To get there, the housing minister, Vincent Jeanbrun, used Article 44, paragraph 2, of the Constitution to set aside, without debate, hundreds of sub-amendments tabled by the far-right Rassemblement national, which he called “obstruction”. Several groups accused the government of not doing the same against other filibusters in the past. A political twist: the minister, from the conservative Les Républicains, was personally opposed to the measure, which the government backed, as did the left; parts of the centre, the right and the far right fought it. The scheme therefore remains temporary, and the question of making it permanent will return in two years.

### 05 · MEDIA · France’s online creators must give candidates fair airtime {#item-05}

Arcom, France’s audiovisual and digital communications regulator, announced on Thursday 19 November that the main video creators on YouTube and social media will, for the first time, have to follow the rules on political pluralism during the 2027 presidential election, as television and radio already do. Its chair, Martin Ajdari, pointed to audiences that sometimes exceed those of traditional channels. From 16 March to 4 April, candidates and their supporters must receive “fair presentation and access”, in line with their standing; from 5 to 30 April, equal speaking time and airtime. Creators will be brought in on three criteria, content, impact and regularity: a channel devoted to gaming, cooking or sport, with no regular forays into public debate, is outside the scope. For breaches, Arcom can issue warnings, then formal notices, then fines for repeated failures. Audio podcasts are not covered. Why it matters: for the first time, the regulator is treating some creators as publishers, with the obligations that come with it.

## Explainer

### 07 · Leasehold {#item-07}

**Why it's in the news.** On Thursday 19 November the government introduced the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill in Parliament, setting a timetable for ending leasehold in England and Wales, where around five million homes are held this way, according to the BBC.

**What it is.** In England most flats are sold as leasehold: the buyer does not get the building or the land, only the right to occupy the flat for a limited number of years under a lease. The freeholder keeps ownership of the building and the ground, collects an annual ground rent and passes on service charges over which residents have little say. The bill makes commonhold the norm, as in most other countries: residents jointly own and run the building, with no ground rent and no expiring lease. The timetable: a modernised commonhold around 2029, then a ban on selling most new flats as leasehold around 2031; a £250-a-year cap on ground rents in 2028, six months after the law passes, falling to a nominal “peppercorn” after forty years. Converting an existing building would need the support of 50% of leaseholders, rather than all of them as now. For example, a flat owner in Oldham quoted by the BBC will see her ground rent go from £150 to £300 in January, on top of more than £4,000 a year in service charges. The limits: campaigners fear a two-tier system between buyers of new flats and existing leaseholders, and the Residential Freehold Association, which represents the largest freeholders, calls the bill “an unprecedented abuse of property rights” that it says could cost pension funds invested in ground rents billions. The bill still has to pass both Houses.

## Technology & science

### 08 · SECURITY · Ruby: seven memory bugs, fixed in the interpreter {#item-08}

The Ruby language team released versions 4.0.10, 3.4.13 and 3.3.14 on Wednesday 18 November, fixing seven vulnerabilities in its regular-expression engine and in string case conversion (turning text into upper or lower case). For several of them, it says it knows of proofs of concept showing remote code execution is possible “in some scenarios”. The most discussed, dubbed “ruby2ßhell” by the security firm Veria Labs, is a one-byte buffer overflow: most single-byte encodings have no capital ß, so upper-casing a “ß” writes two letters, “SS”, where the code has only checked room for one. UTF-8 strings are not affected. According to Veria Labs, the flaw can be reached without authentication in the default configuration of the Rails and Sinatra web frameworks, but not others; the firm will publish its proof of concept in a week. Keep the layers apart: the bug is in the Ruby interpreter, not in Rails, and the fix is to upgrade Ruby itself, or the software that bundles it. At 18.30 UTC on Thursday, Veria Labs still listed GitLab 19.6.0, GitHub Enterprise Server 3.22.5 and Chatwoot 4.20.0 as shipping a vulnerable Ruby, and Mastodon, OpenProject and Discourse as already fixed. Ruby 3.2 and older, which have reached end of life, will get no fix, and CVE identifiers had not yet been assigned.

### 09 · DATABASES · PostgreSQL 19: clean up a table without stopping it {#item-09}

The open-source relational database PostgreSQL reached version 19 on Thursday 19 November. The most practical change for the people who run it is the REPACK command. Heavily updated tables build up unused space, known as “bloat”, which slows queries down; until now VACUUM FULL or CLUSTER reclaimed it but locked the table while they worked. REPACK (CONCURRENTLY) does it without blocking reads or writes, and replaces both commands, which stay available for compatibility. The pg\_plan\_advice extension lets you pin the query planner’s choices, which can shift as data changes, sometimes to a slower plan; its companion pg\_stash\_advice applies that advice automatically, without touching application code. For set-ups that read from standby servers, the WAIT FOR LSN command lets a session wait until its own writes have been replayed there before reading; and logical replication no longer needs a server restart to switch on. The limit: LWN notes that several features originally planned for this release were removed before it shipped.

### 10 · ANDROID · Developer verification goes global in January {#item-10}

Google will start enforcing Android developer verification in every region from January 2027, Ars Technica reports. Once it is live, a Google-certified phone will refuse to install an app from outside the Play Store if its developer has not registered, which requires official ID and a $25 fee. Google presents it as protection against malware; it has applied since September in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand, and the company says apps already registered account for more than 99% of Android app installs worldwide. After criticism, Google added a free hobbyist account limited to 50 installs, and an “advanced flow” that lets users install anything, at the cost of stern warnings and a 24-hour wait. Ars Technica points to the limits: developers in sanctioned countries will not be able to verify, and some installs will require a connection to Google’s servers. The big third-party app stores, including Samsung’s, Xiaomi’s and Oppo’s, have agreed to list only verified apps.

### 11 · AI · Genie 4: Google sells playable worlds generated on the fly {#item-11}

On Thursday 19 November Google made Genie 4 generally available in the Gemini API for developers: a model that generates, in real time, a video world you can move through, according to the API’s release notes. You start from text, a reference image or both, then move with the keyboard and change the scene mid-session with written instructions; the stream runs at 24 frames per second in 16:9, with synchronised stereo sound. Video costs $17.50 per million output tokens, which Google puts at about 10 cents a second. Why it matters: “world models”, which simulate an interactive environment, are moving from research demos to a product billed by the second. The limits: these are Google’s own specifications, with no independent evaluation yet, and the offer is limited to the paid tier.

### 12 · What if the horse, not wheat, made the state? {#item-12}

In Aeon (16 November), Lhamsuren Munkh-Erdene takes on an idea that has become almost common sense, from Jared Diamond to James C. Scott and even David Graeber and David Wengrow: that grain farming and settled life gave birth to the state. For him the decisive condition is transport beyond human legs, pack animals, horses, boats, which lets people and resources be concentrated and territory conquered; he points to the Comanche empire, built without agriculture, and to steppe powers such as the Mongols. He also argues that the grain paradigm owes much to the Cold War and to political quarrels over China’s origins. Why click: a bold thesis that unsettles books many of us have read. The limit: a polemical essay that lumps very different authors together, and whose argument still has to face the specialists.

### 13 · Hobbes, philosopher of curiosity {#item-13}

In The Conversation (19 November, in French), Philippe Silberzahn, a professor at EM Lyon Business School, rereads Thomas Hobbes’s Leviathan (1651), known for its bleak view of humanity, from an unexpected angle: curiosity, an “appetite for knowledge”, is what sets humans apart from animals. Born of anxiety about the future, it drives us to seek the causes of things, at the risk of gnawing at anyone who looks too far ahead, like the eagle at Prometheus’s liver. Hobbes distinguishes two ways of thinking: looking for the means to a desired effect, which animals can do too, and imagining everything an available means might produce. Why click: a short, clear read that draws on the philosopher Kathryn Tabb’s work. The limit: the author, a specialist in entrepreneurship, pulls Hobbes towards a theory of innovation, one reading among others.

### 14 · An eight-page zine, folded by your browser {#item-14}

A single A4 sheet, folded three times and slit down the middle, makes an eight-page booklet with no staples or glue; the puzzle is the layout, because the pages print in an odd order, with the top row upside down. [fanzines.app](https://fanzines.app/), Wilson Tovar’s tool, lets you lay out each page the right way up, with photos and text, then places everything on the sheet and produces a 300 DPI PDF. Everything stays in the browser, with no photos uploaded to a server, according to its maker, who has just added spread-based editing and printer-safe margins. Why click: a small, well-thought-out tool for a very tangible pleasure, ready for the weekend. The limit: a personal project, available in English and Spanish.
