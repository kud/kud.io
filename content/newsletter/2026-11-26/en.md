### 01 · SPAIN · A snap election, and the housing crisis that forced it {#item-01}

Spaniards vote on Sunday 29 November, eight months ahead of schedule. The Socialist prime minister, Pedro Sánchez, in power since 2018, dissolved parliament on 5 October, three days after the Congress of Deputies rejected two decrees strengthening tenants’ protections, with the votes of the People’s Party (PP, centre right), Vox (far right) and Junts, Carles Puigdemont’s Catalan pro-independence party. He is betting that the housing crisis works in his favour. The polls suggest otherwise.

**The idea.** Housing has become a central political question because the gap between rents and wages has widened. According to Cyril Trépier, a researcher at Neoma Business School, writing in The Conversation, rents have risen 40% in five years. France 24 gives a sense of scale: about €1,170 a month for 50 square metres in Madrid, according to the Idealista property portal, against an average gross salary of €2,460, according to the National Statistics Institute. Young Spaniards leave their parents’ home at around 30 on average, compared with about 26 in the rest of the European Union, according to Eurostat. There is arithmetic too: the governing left (the Socialists and Sumar) holds only 147 of the 350 seats in Congress, with no majority to pass a budget or its housing measures.

**Key dates**

- **23 September 2026**: Maricarmen Abascal, 87, is evicted in Madrid from the flat she had lived in for 71 years, according to France 24; she dies in hospital on 7 October. Her case sets off protests across the country.
- **2 October**: Congress rejects the two tenant decrees.
- **5 October**: parliament is dissolved; the election, due in July 2027, is brought forward.
- **11 November**: a general strike, the first since 2012, according to The Conversation.
- **29 November**: polling day.

**Did you know?** Housing is also helping Vox, which proposes deregulating land use, cutting taxes and cracking down on squatting. According to figures from the Centre for Sociological Research (CIS) cited by France 24, Vox has 23.2% of voting intentions among 18- to 24-year-olds, ahead of the Socialists (22%), up from 15.4% in that age group in 2023.

Alberto Núñez Feijóo’s PP leads, on 31.5% according to France 24, and polls do not give it an outright majority on its own: with Vox, it could govern. The model under discussion is Andalusia, run by a PP-Vox coalition since the regional election in May, whose agreement promises priority for benefits and social housing to people with longstanding ties to the region or to Spain, Al Jazeera reports. Last month, the two parties backed a non-binding motion calling for Spanish citizens to be given priority for subsidised housing. Feijóo says he would prefer a minority government backed by Vox, without ruling out a coalition. On the left, Sumar is now part of a new alliance, the Frente Amplio, led by the former Barcelona mayor Ada Colau. The voting system, the D’Hondt method applied province by province, penalises the third and fourth parties in thinly populated provinces. Pedro Sánchez sums up his campaign in a line: the PP and Vox “only agree when it comes to cutting back rights”.

**Further reading**: Cyril Trépier’s piece in The Conversation also covers the campaign’s other issues, from rail crashes to corruption cases.

### 02 · HEALTH · The generic HIV prevention jab reaches Africa, at $40 a year {#item-02}

The first generic doses of lenacapavir, an injection that protects against HIV for six months, arrived in Zambia on Wednesday 25 November; Uganda is due to receive supplies in the coming days and Zimbabwe in early 2027, according to the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria, which is paying for them. Lenacapavir targets the protein shell around the virus and stops it multiplying. It is a form of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP): medication taken before any contact with the virus to prevent infection. In a 2024 trial in Uganda and South Africa involving more than 5,000 adolescent girls and young women, none of those who received the injection was infected, the BBC notes; the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended it since July 2025. The price changes the scale: Gilead’s original version costs about $28,000 a year in the United States, while the generic is expected to cost about $40, according to the BBC. According to the Global Fund, more than 189,000 people in ten countries have already had a first dose, 24 countries should have supplies by the end of the year, and the goal, shared with the US government, is to reach at least 3 million people by the end of 2028. Two injections a year spare people a daily pill, which can carry a stigma. Funding remains the question: the rollout comes as cuts to US foreign aid threaten HIV prevention in countries that rely heavily on it.

### 03 · CITIES · In Manhattan, a park raised three metres against the sea {#item-03}

On Manhattan’s east side, between 25th Street and Montgomery Street, New York City is finishing a major climate-adaptation project: the East Side Coastal Resiliency project, costing $1.45bn. Rather than build a plain sea wall in front of East River Park, the city raised parts of the park by 8 to 10 feet (2.4 to 3 metres), above a concrete wall 2 feet (60 centimetres) thick, backed by berms and movable floodgates. It is designed to withstand water about 16.5 feet (5 metres) above sea level, plus a 2-foot margin, and should protect around 110,000 residents. “If the water rises, it will go on the steps; the park is now too high to be flooded,” an official at the city’s Department of Design and Construction told Inside Climate News. Work began in 2020 and is due to finish in 2027. The cost is visible: the felling of mature trees, contested since the project’s 2018 redesign, the loss of access to the park during the works and the demolition of the fireboat house, home to an ecology centre. About 600 of a planned 2,000 new trees have been planted. Some residents argue the same protection could have been had without destroying the park; the city admits it did not explain the scale of the tree removal well enough. It is a textbook case: protecting a city from the sea sometimes means sacrificing, for a while, what makes it liveable.

### 04 · GAMES · Rockstar staff vote down union recognition {#item-04}

Staff at Rockstar Games, the studio behind _Grand Theft Auto VI_, have rejected a union recognition agreement, with only 25% of votes in favour, according to The Verge. The vote, held from 12 to 26 November as the game launched, concerned the IWGB (Independent Workers Union of Great Britain), a British union. A win would have set up a framework for collective bargaining on pay, overtime, bonuses, leave and pensions, and a right to be consulted on the use of AI and on lay-offs. Without it, Rockstar has no obligation to meet union representatives. The background matters: last year the studio fired 31 IWGB members, which the union called the most blatant case of union-busting in the industry’s history; the case is before a UK employment tribunal, which has yet to rule. The union says hundreds of staff voted for it and that it will keep organising. Rockstar’s management, quoted by Bloomberg, sees the result as the team’s support for “direct relationships”. Unionised staff can still push for change, but only informally.

### 05 · PHILOSOPHY · Can an AI be conscious? Thomas Nagel says no {#item-05}

Thomas Nagel, who in 1974 wrote a famous essay, “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?”, has just won the Berggruen Prize for philosophy, worth $1m. In an interview with Wired, he dismissed the idea that today’s AI systems are conscious, MIT Technology Review reports. His arguments, according to that piece: producing language does not show that anything is felt; looking only at information processing ignores the difference between a biological brain and silicon circuits; reproducing what a brain does is not reproducing what it experiences. The question is not academic for Anthropic, which since April 2025 has run a research programme on “model welfare”, noting that there is no scientific consensus on whether current or future AI systems could be conscious. According to the San Francisco Standard, its flotation prospectus lists model welfare among the risks to the business. Philosophers are divided: some found Nagel’s certainty misplaced, others read him as saying that being alive is a condition of consciousness. A New York doctoral student puts a simple objection: why would we assume a model that chats is conscious, but not one that recognises faces or drives a car? In a 2020 survey of 1,092 philosophers, 39% expected some future AI systems to be conscious.

### 06 · A drained peatland {#item-06}

**Why it matters.** Talk of global warming turns to fossil fuels, and rightly so. A study published in Nature by an MIT-led team measures, at global scale, the effect of an old and quiet practice: draining peatlands for farming or other uses. It would account for 2.7% (between 1.8 and 3.6%) of the energy imbalance humanity has caused since the industrial era, from just 0.4 to 0.5% of the world’s land.

**What it is.** A peatland is waterlogged ground, such as a bog or a fen, where dead plants pile up without fully rotting, for lack of oxygen. Peatlands hold some 600 billion tonnes of carbon this way, more than all the world’s living vegetation, Ars Technica notes. The catch: some of that carbon escapes as methane, a stronger greenhouse gas than CO₂. Digging ditches to drain them lets oxygen in: bacteria break down the peat and release large amounts of CO₂, while methane emissions fall. The researchers reconstructed the balance since 1800 as “radiative forcing”, the measure of the energy imbalance that gases impose on the Earth: +83 milliwatts per square metre from CO₂, −9 from avoided methane, +73 in all, within an uncertainty range of 48 to 99. More than half of that CO₂ comes from Europe and nearly a quarter from South-East Asia, whose contribution has only jumped in recent decades. The key point is inertia: a drained peatland keeps emitting for as long as it stays dry. If nothing changes, the effect would almost double by 2100; ambitious rewetting could hold it in check.

### 07 · SOFTWARE · Anthropic rewrites its API in Rust, with AI doing the translating {#item-07}

On 25 November, Anthropic published an account of moving its Messages API, the gateway through which customers and the company’s own apps reach its Claude models, from Python to Rust, a compiled language known for speed and safe memory handling. Built in 2023 as a simple relay, the service now validates requests, builds prompts, splits text into tokens, runs safety screening and parses responses, while traffic has grown roughly 1,800-fold since early 2024, the company says. A first, partial attempt in March rewrote only the response parsing: it cut CPU use by 8 to 10%, but every token crossed the boundary between the two languages three times. So the team rewrote the lot; public traffic moved to Rust in September. According to the post, Claude produced a first version of the core in about a week; the real work was proving the new version behaved exactly like the old one. The 700 or so integration tests were too tied to Python, and an audit found more than 100 assertions weakened in the port. The method: run Rust “in the shadow” of real traffic, replaying the same model output through both versions, and comparing only where and by how much they differed, without reading the content of requests. In April, Rust passed 98% of the tests but matched only about a quarter of real inputs; agreement reached 99.6% by August. Anthropic reports about 70% less CPU per request in one region, more than 80% less memory, and median time to first token down about 20% on a narrower sample. The post also flags a limit: an agent proposed a shortcut that satisfied the check without being correct, and an engineer rejected it. These are the company’s figures, not independent measurements.

### 08 · Three browsers, one tab {#item-08}

Side by Side opens the same address in Firefox, Chromium and Epiphany, which is to say the web’s three main rendering engines (Gecko, Blink and WebKit), each in its own Linux virtual machine running inside your tab. The trick is a Linux kernel compiled to WebAssembly, running the real browsers downloaded from the NixOS distribution’s package cache, with no server and nothing to install. You can click and scroll in each pane as in a real browser, add NetSurf or switch to a phone-sized view, and the page address keeps the whole comparison so you can share it. The honest limits: you need a computer with at least 8 GB of memory, the first launch takes one to three minutes, and traffic goes through a public relay that can see which sites are visited.

### 09 · Kierkegaard, and the love that sets you free {#item-09}

How do you tell love from a passion that traps you? Ross Channing Reed, who teaches philosophy at Missouri University of Science and Technology, rereads Søren Kierkegaard. In _Either/Or_ (1843), the Danish philosopher contrasts “aesthetic” love, the kind you fall into, which turns the other person into a cure for your own emptiness, with “ethical” love, chosen and built on trust and respect, which he develops in _Works of Love_ (1847). His paradox: it is commitment, not impulse, that sets you free, and the greatest good one person can do for another is to set them free. A clear reading of a difficult writer, though the piece, drawn from the author’s counselling work, sometimes drifts towards life advice.
