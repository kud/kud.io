## Today’s focus

### 01 · AUTONOMOUS WEAPONS · Killer robots: no treaty, and two more years to write rules {#item-01}

The 128 states party to the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), the treaty covering weapons deemed excessively injurious or indiscriminate, broke up in Geneva early on Saturday 21 November after a night of talks, without a mandate to negotiate a legally binding ban on lethal autonomous weapons systems: the “killer robots” able to kill without a human taking the final decision to fire. Instead they agreed, in the words of the conference’s Swiss chair, Julien Thoni, quoted by DW, “to launch the elaboration of an international instrument” on these weapons “over the next two years”. According to Swissinfo, government experts are to discuss further elements of an agreement by 2028, then propose whether or not it should lead to a binding treaty. Nearly 100 countries had called for negotiations on a binding text, as had the UN Secretary-General, António Guterres, Pope Leo XIV and the Red Cross, DW notes; countries that have the technology, among them the United States, Russia and Israel, opposed it. Julien Thoni hailed “concrete results” in a “deeply divided world”. Stop Killer Robots, a coalition of more than 300 organisations, called the mandate “watered-down”: in its account, more than 95 states supported a treaty, and “a small number of states” weakened the text in the final hours, “introducing uncertainty about the legal status of the final document”.

**The idea**

Why can a handful of countries hold everything up? Because the CCW works by consensus: the report of its group of governmental experts, dated 3 September, itself recommended that the Convention’s review conference “decides by consensus” on its future work. A single state can stop a mandate. On substance, that same report contains the nearest thing to a shared definition: a lethal autonomous weapon system “can be characterized” as a combination of weapons and technological components that, together, “can identify, select, and engage a target with force, without intervention by a human being operating the system”. The detail matters. A human programming a set of potential targets does not take a weapon outside that definition; it falls outside only when the people planning or carrying out the attack determine the specific target and the timing of the strike. The text also states that international humanitarian law, the law of armed conflict, applies fully to these weapons and imposes obligations “on States, parties to armed conflict and individuals, not on machines”. Hence the requirement for “control and human judgement” to respect the principles of distinction between civilians and combatants, proportionality and precaution.

**Did you know?**

The disagreement is not only about the timetable. For supporters of a treaty, time is short: Stop Killer Robots says such systems have already been tested or deployed, including in the war in Ukraine, a claim that remains the coalition’s own. For the states developing these weapons, a non-binding instrument leaves room for their programmes. The experts’ report also says its characterisation is offered “without prejudice” to future modification: the line between an “automatic” and an “autonomous” weapon remains open to negotiation.

## The essentials

### 02 · CLIMATE · COP31 stuck on fossil fuels, with suspension a real risk {#item-02}

The UN climate conference in Antalya, Turkey, has run more than 24 hours past its scheduled end with no deal in sight, the BBC reported on Saturday. The sticking point is unchanged: the draft no longer refers to reducing demand for fossil fuels, a reference removed under pressure from producers led by Saudi Arabia. A group led by France and Panama, with island states and several EU countries (Spain, Belgium, Sweden, Finland), wants it back: “It is not acceptable to have a COP or a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels,” said Panama’s environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro. France’s minister for ecological transition, Monique Barbut, criticised the text’s references to carbon capture and storage, which she sees as a licence to keep burning oil and gas. The Africa Group also wants more money for adaptation: the goal of raising it to $120bn by 2035, present in earlier versions, has gone from the text. According to the BBC, one proposal would keep fossil fuels out of the text in exchange for Saudi Arabia and other producers pledging to get 35% of their energy consumption from electricity by 2035. The risk: delegations leaving, taking the meeting below the number of countries needed to adopt a deal. The conference would then be suspended, which has happened only once since 1995.

### 03 · JUSTICE · Zecler case: three officers convicted, with racism upheld {#item-03}

Three police officers were convicted in Paris on Friday 20 November of beating the music producer Michel Zecler in his studio in 2020, with racism upheld as an aggravating circumstance, the BBC reports. They were also found guilty of signing a false police report claiming that the producer, now 47, had violently resisted arrest. The studio’s CCTV had shown some 30 punches, kicks and baton blows against very little resistance. All three received five-year sentences, partly suspended: two years to serve for Aurélien Lapersonne, eighteen months for Philippe Tondeux, and one year under an electronic tag for Pierre Plantier. In court they admitted the beating and the false report but denied any racist motive; the court accepted Michel Zecler’s testimony that they had used a racial slur, because, the BBC notes, everything else he said had proved verifiable. The producer told the court that without the video he would have appeared as a defendant rather than a victim. The verdict comes as police again face criticism over the use of force at recent protests.

## Technology & science

### 04 · AI & SECURITY · Two million traps to follow prompts through the AI supply chain {#item-04}

What happens to a prompt sent to an AI model through a “router”, one of the services that resell access to many models behind a single interface and pick the cheapest provider? To find out, HD Moore, chief research officer at the cybersecurity firm Dragos, sent about two million prompts between July and November, each containing a unique decoy, a secret web address or a working key to an empty Amazon Web Services account, then watched where these “canaries” resurfaced. His findings, published on Thursday: evidence that at least three providers were compromised, with stolen keys tested sometimes within hours and used, according to Ars Technica, “tens of thousands” of times; resellers on an inference marketplace keeping prompts through ordinary logging features, without exploiting any flaw; and providers that did not honour privacy settings. Moore’s example: a Chinese AI lab’s systems visited the secret addresses in more than half the prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds of those marked “zero data retention”, even when those models were resold by US hosts. The mechanism is simple: encryption protects a prompt in transit, but each intermediary decrypts it to route it. The practical lesson: a privacy flag is a promise, not a control, and keys and secrets have no place in a prompt.

### 05 · MATHEMATICS · Riemann: the Lean community certifies the formal proof; understanding it is still to come {#item-05}

On Friday we reported the proof of the Riemann hypothesis announced by Levent Alpöge, a mathematician employed by Anthropic, written with the Claude AI in Lean, a language in which every step of an argument is checked by computer. A day later, the Lean and Mathlib communities (Mathlib being the large library of formalised mathematics) published a joint statement, endorsed by the organisations behind Lean and Mathlib: they “certify” that both the statement and the proof are correct. Their checks answer Friday’s doubts: the library, 17 million lines long, depends only on Mathlib; dozens of people have compiled it and obtained the same proof file; two independent tools confirm that the theorem proved is exactly the statement of the Riemann hypothesis already in Mathlib; and several checkers, including Lean’s official kernel and two checkers themselves proved unable to accept a false proof, have validated it. What the certification does not tell us is why the proof works. According to the BBC, its complete text in Lean runs to more than 250,000 pages, and Terence Tao, one of the best-known mathematicians alive, warned on Friday that “the digestion process will take some time”. The president of the Clay Mathematics Institute, Martin Bridson, quoted by DW, calls it an announcement “of historic significance” but warns that the journey to “a place of real understanding will be long and difficult”.

### 06 · SECURITY · GPGME: one crafted key is enough to corrupt memory {#item-06}

The GnuPG project released GPGME 2.3.1 on 20 November. GPGME is the library through which many programs use OpenPGP encryption without calling the gpg tool directly, and every earlier version has a critical flaw (CVE-2026-129975): an OpenPGP key carrying a crafted, very long user ID triggers a double free, the same block of memory released twice. According to the advisory, it can be used to exfiltrate data from an application, for example by sending it signed data; remote code execution “can’t be ruled out”, though it has not been demonstrated. The flaw is in the library, not in gpg itself, but reaches whatever embeds it: Gpg4win, GnuPG Desktop, “most mail clients”, many XMPP clients, Samba and Flatpak. The fix: update GPGME, or Gpg4win to 5.1.2, and rebuild statically linked programs; a patch is provided for older versions. Reported by GitLab on 12 November, the flaw was due to be disclosed on the 25th; it came out early because the Ruby2Shell bug forced GitLab to release its own updates.

### 07 · Looking poor, one more injustice {#item-07}

In Psyche (12 November), Giacomo Floris, a lecturer in moral and political philosophy at Leiden University, names a wrong rarely taken seriously: “aesthetic injustice”, being treated with less respect because of how you look. Unhoused people are, he argues, its foremost victims, because their appearance carries the marks of their condition, what the sociologist Erving Goffman called “stigma symbols”. His conclusion: society owes them access to haircuts, showers and clean clothes, not as charity but as a matter of justice. Why click: a clear Rawlsian argument that moves a question of hygiene into one of equality. The limit: it is a normative essay that draws on studies rather than adding any.

### 08 · Why is there something rather than nothing? {#item-08}

Leibniz asked the question in 1714, convinced that nothing would have been “simpler and easier”. In Aeon (12 November), the philosopher Andrew T. Brenner of Hong Kong Baptist University turns the argument round: if something simple enough exists and explains enough truths about the world, a reality where something exists could be simpler than one where nothing does, and our astonishment would dissolve. He is open about the idea’s fragility: it assumes reality somehow “strives” for simplicity, and leaves untouched the mystery of why it would. Why click: a long essay of serious metaphysics, from Hempel to Ockham’s razor, that says honestly what it does not solve.

### 09 · PostgreSQL extensions, written in Go {#item-09}

gopgext turns ordinary Go code into a PostgreSQL extension: annotate a function, and the tool uses TinyGo, a Go compiler for small targets, to generate the C glue, the install script and the control file. The standard library works, common types (strings, arrays, JSON, UUIDs, timestamps) map to SQL types, and you can write triggers, aggregates, index operator classes or foreign data wrappers; ports of official modules such as pgcrypto and hstore pass, according to the author, their original regression tests. Why click: an elegant way to open PostgreSQL extensions to developers who do not write C. The limit, stated at the top of the project: it is experimental and not for production, because a bug runs inside the server, where it can crash it or corrupt data.
