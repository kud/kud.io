# Editorial rules

## What an edition is

One calm evening read, about 7 to 10 minutes, that explains what genuinely
mattered today, why it matters, and adds a few worthwhile discoveries. It is
written for any curious reader, and it should be pleasant enough to be the
editor's own daily briefing.

It is a selection, not a feed. An article that turned up in a source has no
right to appear.

## Principles

- **Selection over exhaustiveness.** Five things explained beat twenty listed.
  If a day is quiet, the edition is short.
- **Subject over article.** Write about what happened, not about a
  publication's piece about it. "The court ruled…", never "This article
  explains…".
- **Research beyond discovery.** Feeds and publications surface candidates.
  Once a subject is shortlisted, read further: more than one account, and the
  primary material where it exists.
- **Primary sources first.** Prefer the ruling, the study, the filing, the
  report, the official release or dataset, then the journalism that reads it.
- **Cross-read what matters.** A major claim is checked against a second
  reliable account or a primary source before it goes in.
- **Context is the job.** Say what led here, why it matters now and what it may
  change. A reader who missed the last six months should still follow.
- **Evidence over confidence.** Keep three registers apart: established fact,
  attributed claim ("the ministry says…"), and genuine uncertainty. Never
  upgrade a claim into a fact for the sake of a cleaner sentence.
- **No filler.** No routine launches, funding rounds, trend pieces, listicles,
  or a second summary of something already covered.
- **Balance without false balance.** Cover the range of the day: world affairs
  and geopolitics, politics and society, economics and public policy, climate
  and environment, science, technology. The mix follows the news, not a quota.
  Weight is given by evidence and consequence, not by who shouted loudest.

## Shape of an edition

Sections appear in this order. Any of them can be absent on a given day; an
empty section is never padded to exist.

| Section                                        | What goes in it                                                                       | Typical size                  |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| L’essentiel / The brief                        | the day's genuinely important developments                                            | 5–7 items                     |
| En profondeur / In depth                       | one subject developed further, when one deserves it                                   | 0–1 item, 300–500 words       |
| Technologie & science / Technology & science   | meaningful changes in research and technology, not product news                       | 1–3 items                     |
| En bref / In brief                             | secondary facts worth knowing quickly                                                 | 2–5 items, 1–2 sentences each |
| Trouvailles / Finds                            | public tools, sites, projects, essays or other discoveries worth someone's time       | 2–4 items                     |
| À surveiller / Watch next                      | what is likely to move tomorrow or soon: a vote, a ruling, a release, a deadline      | 1–4 items                     |

An optional lede of one or two sentences opens each language: the day in a
breath. It is also the teaser used to announce the edition, so it names
subjects rather than mood.

The whole edition, in one language, lands around 1,800 to 2,500 words.

## Writing an item

Every item answers, in this order and in plain prose (no labels):

1. **What happened.** The fact, with who, where and when. One or two sentences.
   This first paragraph is reused as the item's summary, so it must stand on
   its own.
2. **Why it matters.** The consequence for people, institutions or the
   argument it settles.
3. **Context.** What preceded it, and the number or precedent that gives it
   scale.
4. **What is uncertain.** What is disputed, unverified or not yet known. Leave
   it out only when nothing is.

A brief item is roughly 80 to 150 words; an "in brief" item is one or two
sentences; a find says what it is, who it is for and why it is worth a look.

Each item ends with its sources (see [`FORMAT.md`](./FORMAT.md)): the primary
document where there is one, plus the reliable accounts the item relied on.
Label each source by its publication or document name, not its headline.

Titles say what happened: "EU agrees methane limits for imported gas", not
"Big news on methane".

## Two languages, one edition

Every edition exists in natural French and natural British English.

- **Same facts.** Same subjects, same order, same item numbers, same section
  for each item, same sources. The two languages are two renditions of one
  factual edition.
- **Natural prose, not a translation.** Each language reads as if written in
  it. Idiom, rhythm and sentence order may differ; facts, figures, names,
  attributions and hedges may not. A hedge in one language is a hedge in the
  other.
- **French.** Typographic conventions of French press: non-breaking space
  before `: ; ? !`, « guillemets », numbers like `1 200` and `3,5 %`.
- **English.** British spelling and usage (colour, organise, programme, per
  cent in prose, 3.5% in figures), dates as 14 November 2026.
- **Names.** Use the established name in each language (Pékin / Beijing,
  Royaume-Uni / United Kingdom); keep names of publications, organisations and
  products as they are.
- **Source labels may be localised**; the source URLs may not differ.

## Tone

Calm, precise and warm. Short sentences when the facts are hard. No
sensationalism, no sarcasm about people, no "shocking", "slams" or "game
changer". Humour, if any, belongs to the Finds.

## Before an edition is marked ready

- Every substantive claim can be traced to a listed public source.
- Both languages carry the same items, numbers, sections and source URLs.
- Nothing in it breaks [`SAFETY.md`](./SAFETY.md).
- The repository's newsletter validator passes on it.
- It has been reread once, start to finish, in each language.
