# Editorial rules

## What an edition is

One calm evening read, about 7 to 10 minutes, that explains what genuinely
mattered today, why it matters, and adds a few worthwhile discoveries. It is
written for any curious reader, and it should be pleasant enough to be the
editor's own daily briefing.

It is an **explanatory daily brief**: a short welcome, one subject explained
properly, the day's essential stories told in dense factual paragraphs, a few
discoveries worth a click, and a friendly word to close. It explains as much
as it reports: a reader who missed the last six months should still follow.

## Editorial line

The newsletter has a stance, and says so. It chooses and frames its subjects
from a progressive perspective: social and ecological justice, the public
interest, and structural explanations rather than purely individual ones.
That stance shapes what we look at; it never shapes what we report as true.

- **Facts first.** Primary sources, attribution and cross-reading apply to
  every subject, including the ones we care about most. A claim we would like
  to be true gets the same scrutiny as one we would not.
- **No propaganda, from any side.** No partisan talking points, no slogans,
  no unverified claims, no selective quotation that changes a meaning.
- **Interested sources are claims to check.** Government and state-media
  statements, lobbying material, corporate communications and campaign
  material are reported as what someone says, attributed, and checked;
  never used as a source of fact on their own.
- **Ideas, with substance.** Essays and ideas pieces earn their place by
  argument and evidence. No self-help, no inspirational fluff, nothing
  sentimental for its own sake.

## Principles

- **Selection over exhaustiveness.** A handful of subjects told properly beat
  twenty listed. If a day is quiet, the edition is short.
- **Explain, don't assume.** Every acronym, institution and technical term is
  defined the first time it appears: "Ofcom, the UK's communications
  regulator", "l'Arcom, l'autorité de régulation de l'audiovisuel".
- **Subject over article.** Write about what happened, not about a
  publication's piece about it. "The court ruled…", never "This article
  explains…".
- **Attribute.** Every claim that is not established fact says whose it is:
  "according to", "the ministry said", "reports", "selon", "a réagi". Keep
  three registers apart: fact, attributed claim, genuine uncertainty. Never
  upgrade a claim into a fact for the sake of a cleaner sentence.
- **Precise.** Exact figures, full dates, named people and institutions, the
  article of the law. "Inflation eased" says less than "inflation fell to 2.1%
  in October, its lowest since 2021".
- **Research beyond discovery.** Feeds and publications surface candidates.
  Once a subject is shortlisted, read further: more than one account, and the
  primary material where it exists.
- **Primary sources first.** Prefer the ruling, the study, the filing, the
  report, the official release or dataset, then the journalism that reads it.
- **Cross-read what matters.** A major claim is checked against a second
  reliable account or a primary source before it goes in.
- **Context and consequence.** Say what led here and what it may change, in a
  sentence or two; save the longer explanation for the focus or the explainer.
- **Neutral, warm, not chatty.** Authoritative without partisan framing or
  hype; friendly in the welcome, the finds and the closing line, plain
  everywhere else.
- **No filler.** No routine launches, funding rounds, trend pieces or a
  second summary of something already covered.
- **Balance, without quotas.** The day's range: world affairs and geopolitics,
  politics and society, economics and public policy, climate and environment,
  science, technology. The mix follows the news.

## Shape of an edition

Sections appear in this order, with the same set in both languages. Only the
welcome is required; any other section is left out on a day it would be
padded.

| Section                                      | What goes in it                                                        | Size                              |
| -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Welcome (`overview`)                         | what is in tonight's edition, said plainly and warmly                  | 1 paragraph, 3–4 sentences        |
| Le sujet du jour / Today's focus             | one subject explained in depth                                         | 0–1 item, 350–550 words           |
| L’essentiel / The essentials                 | the day's important stories, one dense paragraph each                  | 5–8 items, 90–150 words each      |
| C’est quoi ? / Explainer                     | a term, mechanism or institution in the news, explained                | 0–1 item, 150–250 words           |
| Analyse / Analysis                           | second-order effects, tensions, who gains and who loses                | 0–3 paragraphs                    |
| Technologie & science / Technology & science | meaningful changes in research and technology, not product news        | 0–3 items                         |
| Chronologie / Timeline                       | dated steps that put a story in sequence                               | 0–6 bullets                       |
| En bref / In brief                           | secondary facts worth knowing quickly                                  | 0–6 bullets                       |
| Ça vaut un clic / Worth a click              | public sites, tools, essays, projects or oddities worth someone's time | 2–4 items                         |
| À surveiller / Watch next                    | what is likely to move soon: a vote, a ruling, a release, a deadline   | 0–4 items                         |
| Closing                                      | one friendly sentence to end the edition                               | 1 sentence                        |

The whole edition, in one language, lands around 1,800 to 2,500 words.

## The welcome

One paragraph, three or four sentences, that tells the reader what tonight's
edition holds: the focus subject, the main story or two, and the finds. Warm
and plain ("Tonight, we look back at… After the day's news, we explain… And
three finds worth a click."). It is also the teaser used to announce the
edition, so it names subjects, not a mood. Links are optional; any it carries
must also appear under a story.

## The essentials: one story, one paragraph

Each story opens with a **kicker**, a short uppercase topic (CLIMATE, ISRAEL,
NVIDIA, BANK OF ENGLAND), then one dense factual paragraph:

1. **What happened**: who, what, when and where, with precise figures and
   dates. This first sentence or two is reused as the story's summary, so it
   must stand on its own.
2. **Definitions in passing**: every acronym and institution named and
   explained on first mention, without breaking the sentence.
3. **Attribution**: whose claim each claim is, and how the other side
   responded where there is one.
4. **Context**: one or two sentences on what preceded it or why it matters.

Then, optionally, a "Further reading" pointer, and the sources. No headings
inside a story, no bullet points, no opinion.

## Today's focus

One subject the day calls for understanding rather than just knowing. An
opening paragraph says why it matters now, then four parts, each under a bold
label:

- **The idea**: the concept or mechanism, explained from scratch.
- **Key dates**: a short list, each line starting with the year or date in
  bold, then a short headline, then the explanation.
- **Did you know?**: one surprising fact, sourced like everything else.
- **Further reading**: one or two public pieces worth the reader's time.

## Explainer

A term or institution everyone is using and few could define. Two bold
lead-ins: **Why it's in the news.** then **What it is.** Plain words, an
example, sources.

## Worth a click

A short, playful title that is honest about what is behind it: witty, never
clickbait, never a promise the link does not keep. Three or four sentences on
what it is and why it is worth the click. The link itself is the source.

## Analysis, timeline, in brief, watch next

- **Analysis**, when there is something to say beyond the stories: the
  second-order consequence, the contradiction between two policies, who gains
  and who loses. Cite inline at the claim, on the source's short name; every
  cited link also appears under a story.
- **Timeline** bullets read `YYYY-MM-DD — event`, oldest first, each with a
  link.
- **In brief** bullets are one sentence each, each with a link.
- **Watch next** items say what, when, and what would change.

## The closing line

One friendly sentence, no links: a good night, a nudge to come back
tomorrow, a light touch on the day. Never a summary of the edition.

## Sources

Every item ends with its sources (see [`FORMAT.md`](./FORMAT.md)): the
primary document where there is one, plus the reliable accounts the item
relied on. Label each source by its publication or document name, not its
headline.

## Two languages, one edition

Every edition exists in natural French and natural British English.

- **Same facts.** Same sections, same items in the same order with the same
  numbers, same sources, same links. The two languages are two renditions of
  one factual edition.
- **Natural prose, not a translation.** Each language reads as if written in
  it, and the English is as idiomatic as the French. Idiom, rhythm, kickers,
  find titles and sentence order may differ; facts, figures, names,
  attributions and hedges may not. A hedge in one language is a hedge in the
  other. An institution is defined in each language in the way a reader of
  that language needs it.
- **French.** Typographic conventions of French press: non-breaking space
  before `: ; ? !`, « guillemets », numbers like `1 200` and `3,5 %`.
- **English.** British spelling and usage (colour, organise, programme, per
  cent in prose, 3.5% in figures), dates as 14 November 2026.
- **Names.** Use the established name in each language (Pékin / Beijing,
  Royaume-Uni / United Kingdom); keep names of publications, organisations and
  products as they are.
- **Source labels may be localised**; the source URLs may not differ.

## Tone

Explanatory, neutral and precise; warm without being chatty. Short sentences
when the facts are hard. No sensationalism, no sarcasm about people, no
"shocking", "slams" or "game changer". Wit belongs to the finds and the
closing line. The edition carries no byline.

## Before an edition is marked ready

- Every substantive claim can be traced to a listed public source, and every
  link in the welcome and analysis appears under a story.
- Every acronym and institution is defined on first mention, in each language.
- Both languages carry the same sections, items, numbers and links.
- Nothing in it breaks [`SAFETY.md`](./SAFETY.md).
- The repository's newsletter validator passes on it.
- It has been reread once, start to finish, in each language.
