# Editorial rules

## What an edition is

One calm evening read, about 7 to 10 minutes, that explains what genuinely
mattered today, why it matters, and adds a few worthwhile discoveries. It is
written for any curious reader, and it should be pleasant enough to be the
editor's own daily briefing.

It is a **written briefing first**: an essay that pulls the day together,
then the stories it draws on, each with its detail and sources. It is never a
list of articles with a line under each.

## Principles

- **Selection over exhaustiveness.** Four or five subjects explored properly
  beat twenty listed. If a day is quiet, the edition is short.
- **Synthesis over sequence.** Find the threads between events that look
  unrelated: the same policy behind two headlines, the market that moved
  because of a ruling, the climate number that changes a budget argument.
- **Subject over article.** Write about what happened, not about a
  publication's piece about it. "The court ruled…", never "This article
  explains…".
- **Research beyond discovery.** Feeds and publications surface candidates.
  Once a subject is shortlisted, read further: more than one account, and the
  primary material where it exists.
- **Primary sources first.** Prefer the ruling, the study, the filing, the
  report, the official release or dataset, then the journalism that reads it.
- **Cross-read what matters.** A major claim is checked against a second
  reliable account or a primary source before it goes in.
- **Specific, not general.** Exact figures, named people and institutions,
  the article of the law, the date of the vote. "Inflation eased" says less
  than "inflation fell to 2.1% in October, its lowest since 2021".
- **Causal chains.** Say how things work: the decision, its effect, and the
  response it provokes. Context is part of the job: what led here, why now,
  what it may change.
- **Evidence over confidence.** Keep three registers apart: established fact,
  attributed claim ("the ministry says…"), and genuine uncertainty. Never
  upgrade a claim into a fact for the sake of a cleaner sentence, and never
  speculate beyond what the sources support.
- **Forward-looking.** Name the pattern that is forming and the point where
  it could turn.
- **Neutral, not bland.** Authoritative, without partisan framing or hype.
  Weight comes from evidence and consequence, not from who shouted loudest.
- **No filler.** No routine launches, funding rounds, trend pieces or a
  second summary of something already covered.
- **Balance, without quotas.** The day's range: world affairs and geopolitics,
  politics and society, economics and public policy, climate and environment,
  science, technology. The mix follows the news.

## Shape of an edition

Sections appear in this order, with the same set in both languages. Only the
briefing is required; any other section is left out on a day it would be
padded.

| Section                                      | Kind      | What goes in it                                                                 | Size                         |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| L’essentiel du jour / Today’s briefing       | narrative | the day, synthesised: the main themes and how they connect                       | 3–5 paragraphs, 450–700 words |
| Les sujets / The stories                     | items     | the subjects the briefing draws on, one item each                                | 4–7 items                    |
| En profondeur / In depth                     | items     | one subject developed further, when one deserves it                              | 0–1 item, 300–500 words      |
| Analyse / Analysis                           | narrative | second-order effects, tensions, who gains and who loses                          | 2–3 paragraphs, 200–350 words |
| Technologie & science / Technology & science | items     | meaningful changes in research and technology, not product news                  | 1–3 items                    |
| Chronologie / Timeline                       | list      | dated steps that put the main story in sequence                                  | 3–6 bullets                  |
| En bref / In brief                           | list      | secondary facts worth knowing quickly                                           | 3–6 bullets                  |
| Trouvailles / Finds                          | items     | public tools, sites, projects, essays or other discoveries worth someone's time  | 2–4 items                    |
| À surveiller / Watch next                    | items     | what is likely to move soon: a vote, a ruling, a release, a deadline            | 1–4 items                    |

The whole edition, in one language, lands around 1,800 to 2,500 words.

## Writing the briefing

The briefing is the heart of the edition and the part most readers will read
in full. It reads as one piece of journalism, not as a sequence of summaries.

- **Open with the day's most significant development**, or the theme that
  ties the most together. The first two sentences double as the edition's
  teaser, so they name subjects, not a mood.
- **Connect three or four themes** across the stories, and say why they
  belong in the same paragraph. Each paragraph has one idea.
- **Cite inline, as you go.** Each claim links the source it rests on, on the
  source's short name ("Le Monde", "Reuters", "Cour de justice de l'UE") at the
  point of the claim, not stacked at the end of a paragraph. The site shows
  them as ↗ citations. Every source cited in the briefing or the analysis is
  also listed under the story it belongs to: the narrative never carries a
  source that no story carries.
- **Don't** walk through articles one by one ("In X, A reports… Meanwhile, B
  states…"), lean on stock transitions ("Additionally", "On another note",
  "Par ailleurs", "En outre"), or label paragraphs.
- **Don't** introduce a fact that none of the stories supports. The briefing
  synthesises; the stories hold the evidence.

## Writing the analysis

Optional, and only when there is something to say beyond the briefing:
the second-order consequence, the contradiction between two policies, what
changes for whom, who wins and who loses, what to watch. The same citation
rules apply. If it would only restate the briefing, leave it out.

## Writing a story

Every item answers, in this order and in plain prose (no labels):

1. **What happened.** The fact, with who, where and when. One or two sentences.
   This first paragraph is reused as the item's summary, so it must stand on
   its own.
2. **Why it matters.** The consequence for people, institutions or the
   argument it settles.
3. **Context.** What preceded it, and the number or precedent that gives it
   scale.
4. **What is uncertain.** What is disputed, unverified or not yet known. Leave
   it out only when nothing is.

A story is roughly 100 to 180 words; a find says what it is, who it is for and
why it is worth a look; a watch item says what, when, and what would change.

Each item ends with its sources (see [`FORMAT.md`](./FORMAT.md)): the primary
document where there is one, plus the reliable accounts the item relied on.
Label each source by its publication or document name, not its headline.

Titles say what happened: "EU agrees methane limits for imported gas", not
"Big news on methane".

## Timeline and in brief

- **Timeline** bullets read `YYYY-MM-DD — event`, oldest first, each with at
  least one link. Use it when a story only makes sense in sequence.
- **In brief** bullets are one sentence each, each with at least one link:
  facts worth knowing that need no context.

## Two languages, one edition

Every edition exists in natural French and natural British English.

- **Same facts.** Same sections, same stories in the same order with the same
  numbers, same sources, and the same links in the briefing, analysis,
  timeline and in brief. The two languages are two renditions of one factual
  edition.
- **Natural prose, not a translation.** Each language reads as if written in
  it. Idiom, rhythm and sentence order may differ; facts, figures, names,
  attributions and hedges may not. A hedge in one language is a hedge in the
  other.
- **French.** Typographic conventions of French press: non-breaking space
  before `: ; ? !`, « guillemets », numbers like `1 200` and `3,5 %`.
- **English.** British spelling and usage (colour, organise, programme, per
  cent in prose, 3.5% in figures), dates as 14 November 2026.
- **Names.** Use the established name in each language (Pékin / Beijing,
  Royaume-Uni / United Kingdom); keep names of publications, organisations and
  products as they are.
- **Source labels may be localised**; the source URLs may not differ.

## Tone

Calm, precise and warm. Short sentences when the facts are hard. No
sensationalism, no sarcasm about people, no "shocking", "slams" or "game
changer". Humour, if any, belongs to the Finds.

## Before an edition is marked ready

- Every substantive claim can be traced to a listed public source, and every
  link in the briefing and analysis appears under a story.
- Both languages carry the same sections, stories, numbers and links.
- Nothing in it breaks [`SAFETY.md`](./SAFETY.md).
- The repository's newsletter validator passes on it.
- It has been reread once, start to finish, in each language.
